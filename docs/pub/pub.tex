\title{Language Doesn't Matter: Evaluating the Efficacy of Large
Language Models in Analyzing Sentiment in French Textual Data}


\shorttitle{Language Doesn't Matter}


\usepackage{etoolbox}








\authorsnames{Laurence-Olivier M. Foisy,Camille Pelletier,Étienne
Proulx,Yannick Dufresne}





\affiliation{
{Département de science politique, Université Laval}}




\leftheader{Foisy, Pelletier, Proulx and Dufresne}

\date{2026-10-09}


\abstract{Researchers who analyze non-English text with large language
models (LLMs) inherit two rules of thumb. The first is to prompt in
English, because these models learned mostly from English. The second is
to translate the text into English, as dictionary methods long required.
The evidence for both rules is contested. This article tests them on
French. Fifteen open- and closed-weight language models scored 200
manually annotated French news sentences under three conditions: French
prompts on French text, English prompts on French text, and English
prompts on machine-translated text. The English and French Lexicoder
dictionaries served as benchmarks. Prompt language made no meaningful
difference, and equivalence tests rule out any substantial advantage for
English prompts. Translation cut both ways. It improved the dictionary,
but it lowered the LLMs' polarity classification and, relative to the
fully French workflow, their correlation with human judgments. The best
models reached r = 0.81. Every model from about 8B parameters upward
correlated with human judgments more strongly than either dictionary, at
a cost of a few cents per thousand sentences. A 1B model could not
follow the task. For sentiment analysis of French text, a validated
general-purpose LLM now measures better than the Lexicoder dictionary,
which remains useful as a transparent baseline. Prompt language is a
matter of convenience, and the text is best analyzed in the original. }

\keywords{Sentiment Analysis, Large Language Models, Machine
Translation, Prompt Language, French Language, Open-Weight
Models, Computational Social Science}

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Computational text analysis in the social sciences grew up around
English. Its tools, from sentiment dictionaries to topic models, were
built for English text. This dominance has been criticized for
depressing comparative research and for excluding scholarly communities
that work in other languages
(\citeproc{ref-badenThreeGapsComputational2022}{Baden et al., 2022}).
Comparativists coped by translating. Machine translation made it
possible to convert multilingual corpora into English and to apply
English-language tools (\citeproc{ref-devriesNoLongerLost2018}{De Vries
et al., 2018};
\citeproc{ref-lucasComputerAssistedTextAnalysis2015}{Lucas et al.,
2015}). Large language models (LLMs) promise to loosen this constraint.
Used without task-specific training, they match or exceed crowd workers
and expert coders on common annotation tasks
(\citeproc{ref-gilardiChatGPTOutperformsCrowd2023}{Gilardi et al.,
2023}; \citeproc{ref-tornbergLargeLanguageModels2025}{T"ornberg, 2025}).
They also do so, with some loss, in languages other than English
(\citeproc{ref-heseltineLargeLanguageModels2024}{Heseltine \& Clemm Von
Hohenberg, 2024}; \citeproc{ref-rathjeGPTEffectiveTool2024}{Rathje et
al., 2024}).

Yet researchers who use LLMs on non-English text often keep two reflexes
from the English-centered era. The first is to prompt in English. The
justification is that these models are trained mostly on English
(\citeproc{ref-touvron_etal23a}{Touvron et al., 2023}) and that English
instructions have been found to work better even for non-English inputs
(\citeproc{ref-laiChatGPTEnglishComprehensive2023}{Lai et al., 2023};
\citeproc{ref-Lin2022Few}{X. V. Lin et al., 2022}). The second is to
translate the text into English before analysis. This practice comes
from dictionary and bag-of-words methods, and it reappears as
``translate-test'' with LLMs
(\citeproc{ref-Etxaniz2024Multilingual}{Etxaniz et al., 2024}). Whether
either reflex helps, harms, or makes no difference is unsettled. The
evidence comes mostly from reasoning and classification benchmarks in
natural language processing, often with older models and lower-resourced
languages. The social science evidence is thin.

This article tests both reflexes on sentiment analysis, a measurement
task central to political communication research
(\citeproc{ref-vanAtteveldt2021validity}{van Atteveldt et al., 2021};
\citeproc{ref-young_andsoroka12}{Young \& Soroka, 2012}). Sentiment is a
demanding test for translation, because affective meaning is precisely
what translation can alter
(\citeproc{ref-Mohammad2016translation}{Mohammad et al., 2016}). The
study uses 200 manually annotated sentences from French news coverage.
It compares fifteen open- and closed-weight language models under three
conditions: French prompts on French text, English prompts on French
text, and English prompts on machine-translated text. The English and
French versions of the Lexicoder Sentiment Dictionary serve as
benchmarks. Equivalence tests assess the absence of a prompt-language
effect directly, rather than inferring it from non-significance.

The results speak to both reflexes. Prompt language made no meaningful
difference. Translation cut both ways: it improved the dictionary but
hurt the LLMs. The translation strategy that served English-bound tools
does not carry over to models that can read the source language. The
article also answers a practical question: is a researcher working on
French text still better served by a dictionary? On this evidence, no.
Every model from about 8B parameters upward outperformed both the native
French dictionary and the translated English one on correlation with
human judgments, open-weight models included, for a few cents per
thousand sentences. The dictionary retains transparency and stability.
These make it a baseline rather than a primary measure.

\section{Literature Review}\label{literature-review}

\subsection{LLMs as Measurement Instruments in the Social
Sciences}\label{llms-as-measurement-instruments-in-the-social-sciences}

Zero-shot LLM annotation has moved quickly from curiosity to candidate
standard. ChatGPT outperformed crowd workers on relevance, stance,
topic, and frame detection
(\citeproc{ref-gilardiChatGPTOutperformsCrowd2023}{Gilardi et al.,
2023}). GPT-4 outperformed expert coders and supervised classifiers at
identifying the party of politicians from their social media posts in
eleven countries
(\citeproc{ref-tornbergLargeLanguageModels2025}{T"ornberg, 2025}). GPT-4
also closely approximated expert coding of negativity and sentiment in
tweets from the United States, Chile, Germany, and Italy
(\citeproc{ref-heseltineLargeLanguageModels2024}{Heseltine \& Clemm Von
Hohenberg, 2024}). These results have fed broad claims that generative
AI could transform computational social science
(\citeproc{ref-bailCanGenerativeAI2024}{Bail, 2024};
\citeproc{ref-ziemsCanLargeLanguage2024}{Ziems et al., 2024}), chiefly
by removing the need for the thousands of annotated examples that
supervised learning requires
(\citeproc{ref-chaeLargeLanguageModels2026}{Chae \& Davidson, 2026};
\citeproc{ref-laurerLessAnnotatingMore2024}{Laurer et al., 2024}).

A more cautious literature qualifies these claims on three fronts.
First, performance is uneven. Across 25 English benchmarks, zero-shot
LLMs did not outperform the best fine-tuned models on classification
tasks (\citeproc{ref-ziemsCanLargeLanguage2024}{Ziems et al., 2024}).
Across 27 annotation tasks, their performance was ``highly contingent on
both the dataset and the type of annotation task''
(\citeproc{ref-pangakisAutomatedAnnotationGenerative2023}{Pangakis et
al., 2023}). Validation against human coding therefore remains
indispensable (\citeproc{ref-grimmer_stewart13}{Grimmer \& Stewart,
2013}; \citeproc{ref-linNavigatingRisksUsing2026}{H. Lin \& Zhang,
2026}), and it is only as informative as the human gold standard is
reliable (\citeproc{ref-songValidationsWeTrust2020}{Song et al., 2020}).
Second, established tools retain advantages. Dictionaries generalize
across domains and preserve gradations of sentiment
(\citeproc{ref-vanderveenAdvantagesLexiconbasedSentiment2025}{Van Der
Veen \& Bleich, 2025}). However, off-the-shelf dictionaries validate
poorly against human judgment
(\citeproc{ref-vanAtteveldt2021validity}{van Atteveldt et al., 2021}),
and transformer-based models outperform them in German political and
cultural texts (\citeproc{ref-klahnDictionariesLLMsEvaluation2025}{Klähn
et al., 2025};
\citeproc{ref-widmannCreatingComparingDictionary2023}{Widmann \& Wich,
2023}). A domain-specific multilingual classifier can also match GPT-4o
at a fraction of the cost
(\citeproc{ref-mochtakParlasentMappingSentiment2025}{Mochtak et al.,
2025}). Third, reliance on proprietary models threatens reproducibility
(\citeproc{ref-ollionDangersUsingProprietary2024}{Ollion et al., 2024};
\citeproc{ref-spirlingWhyOpensourceGenerative2023}{Spirling, 2023}).
This has prompted calls to justify their use
(\citeproc{ref-palmerUsingProprietaryLanguage2023}{Palmer et al., 2023})
and evidence that open-weight models can be competitive alternatives
(\citeproc{ref-alizadehOpensourceLLMsText2025}{Alizadeh et al., 2025};
\citeproc{ref-klahnDictionariesLLMsEvaluation2025}{Klähn et al., 2025}).

These debates leave the question of language largely implicit. The
broadest evaluations rely on English benchmarks
(\citeproc{ref-ziemsCanLargeLanguage2024}{Ziems et al., 2024}). Studies
that extend to other languages report somewhat lower performance outside
English (\citeproc{ref-heseltineLargeLanguageModels2024}{Heseltine \&
Clemm Von Hohenberg, 2024};
\citeproc{ref-tornbergLargeLanguageModels2025}{T"ornberg, 2025}), but
they do not examine how researchers should interact with models in those
languages. The most extensive multilingual evaluation in the social
sciences covers 12 languages, yet it compared GPT with dictionary
methods on English headlines only
(\citeproc{ref-rathjeGPTEffectiveTool2024}{Rathje et al., 2024}).
Researchers working in other languages must make two choices: the
language of the prompt and the language of the text. Both have been
debated elsewhere.

\subsection{Prompting in English: Pivot Language or Multilingual
Competence?}\label{prompting-in-english-pivot-language-or-multilingual-competence}

The case for prompting in English starts from two observations. LLMs are
trained predominantly on English (\citeproc{ref-touvron_etal23a}{Touvron
et al., 2023}), and they perform less well in other languages,
especially lower-resourced ones
(\citeproc{ref-ahujaMEGAMultilingualEvaluation2023}{Ahuja et al., 2023};
\citeproc{ref-laiChatGPTEnglishComprehensive2023}{Lai et al., 2023}). A
mechanistic account suggests why. In Llama-2 models, intermediate
representations pass through an abstract ``concept space'' that lies
closer to English than to other languages
(\citeproc{ref-wendler_etal24}{Wendler et al., 2024}). If models
effectively reason through English, instructions written in English
should be understood best. Several findings support this pivot-language
view. ChatGPT performed better with English prompts even when the task
and input were in other languages
(\citeproc{ref-laiChatGPTEnglishComprehensive2023}{Lai et al., 2023}).
English templates remedied the unexpectedly low accuracy of prompts
handcrafted by native speakers (\citeproc{ref-Lin2022Few}{X. V. Lin et
al., 2022}). Models fine-tuned on English prompts performed best with
English prompts even on non-English samples
(\citeproc{ref-Muennighoff2023Crosslingual}{Muennighoff et al., 2023}).
Asking models to restate a non-English request in English improved their
multilingual performance (\citeproc{ref-huangNotAllLanguages2023}{Huang
et al., 2023}). English instructions outperformed Arabic ones on average
across eleven Arabic classification tasks
(\citeproc{ref-Kmainasi2025Native}{Kmainasi et al., 2025}).

A competing view emphasizes multilingual competence. Large models show
strong multilingual reasoning abilities even in underrepresented
languages (\citeproc{ref-shiLanguageModelsAre2022}{Shi et al., 2022}).
For capable models, the language of the prompt appears to matter little.
In the Arabic study just cited, differences across prompt languages were
minimal for the strongest model, GPT-4o
(\citeproc{ref-Kmainasi2025Native}{Kmainasi et al., 2025}). GPT-4
Turbo's labels for Indonesian tweets under English and Indonesian
prompts agreed almost perfectly
(\citeproc{ref-rathjeGPTEffectiveTool2024}{Rathje et al., 2024}).
Changing the prompt language alters the length and wording of generated
responses, while their meaning remains largely stable
(\citeproc{ref-nguyen_etal26}{Nguyen et al., 2026}). The evidence on
both sides comes mostly from reasoning and classification benchmarks,
older models, and lower-resourced languages. None of it tests whether
prompt language affects the accuracy of a graded sentiment judgment in a
well-resourced language. The two views imply competing expectations:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{H1a (pivot language).} English prompts yield more accurate
sentiment measurement of French text than French prompts.

\textbf{H1b (multilingual competence).} English and French prompts yield
equivalent performance on French text.
\end{quote}

\subsection{Translating the Text: Bridge or
Loss?}\label{translating-the-text-bridge-or-loss}

The second reflex predates LLMs. Faced with multilingual corpora,
comparative researchers translated texts into English so that
English-language tools could be applied
(\citeproc{ref-lucasComputerAssistedTextAnalysis2015}{Lucas et al.,
2015}). This bridge strategy has been validated repeatedly.
Machine-translated and human-translated texts yield highly similar
term-document matrices and topic models
(\citeproc{ref-devriesNoLongerLost2018}{De Vries et al., 2018}). Results
are robust across translation services
(\citeproc{ref-reberOvercomingLanguageBarriers2019}{Reber, 2019}). A
machine translation of the Lexicoder dictionary captured legislative
conflict across European parliaments
(\citeproc{ref-prokschMultilingualSentimentAnalysis2019}{Proksch et al.,
2019}). For sentiment specifically, translating non-English sentences
into English and applying English methods performed as well as
language-specific methods
(\citeproc{ref-araujoComparativeStudyMachine2020}{Araújo et al., 2020}),
and sentiment analysis of English translations of Arabic posts was
competitive with analysis of the originals
(\citeproc{ref-Mohammad2016translation}{Mohammad et al., 2016}). The
logic has carried into the LLM era. Translating inputs into English
before inference improves the performance of multilingual models
(\citeproc{ref-Etxaniz2024Multilingual}{Etxaniz et al., 2024}). It is
often hard to beat for lower-resourced languages written in non-Latin
scripts (\citeproc{ref-ahujaMEGAMultilingualEvaluation2023}{Ahuja et
al., 2023}), and it appears in applied research
(\citeproc{ref-Venerito2024Large}{Venerito \& Iannone, 2024}).

The opposing view treats translation as a source of loss. Translation
alters sentiment: affective words disappear or are replaced by words of
opposite valence, ambiguous words are mistranslated, and culturally
specific expressions lose their meaning
(\citeproc{ref-Mohammad2016translation}{Mohammad et al., 2016}).
Approaches that avoid translation, such as multilingual dictionaries
(\citeproc{ref-maierMachineTranslationVs2022}{Maier et al., 2022}) and
multilingual sentence embeddings
(\citeproc{ref-lichtCrossLingualClassificationPolitical2023}{Licht,
2023}), yield comparable or more reliable results in some settings.
Crucially, the case for translation was built largely on tools that
cannot read the source language, such as English dictionaries and
bag-of-words models. LLMs can. For them, the supporting evidence comes
from benchmarks whose non-English versions were themselves created by
translation, a setting that may favor translation-based methods, as the
authors note (\citeproc{ref-Etxaniz2024Multilingual}{Etxaniz et al.,
2024}). It does not come from text written natively in the source
language. Again, the two views imply competing expectations:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{H2a (translation as bridge).} Analyzing English machine
translations yields sentiment measurement at least as accurate as
analyzing the French original.

\textbf{H2b (translation as loss).} Analyzing English machine
translations yields less accurate sentiment measurement than analyzing
the French original.
\end{quote}

\subsection{Research Questions}\label{research-questions}

These debates motivate two research questions. \textbf{RQ1:} Does the
language used to interact with the model affect LLM sentiment
measurement of French text? This covers the language of the prompt and
the language of the text through machine translation. H1a--b and H2a--b
offer competing answers. \textbf{RQ2:} Can general-purpose LLMs, open-
and closed-weight,\footnote{``Open source'' traditionally implies access
  aligned with free and open-source software principles. In the LLM
  context, the term usually refers to the public release of model
  weights. ``Open-weight'' describes this condition more accurately and
  is used throughout.} measure sentiment in French text well enough,
without fine-tuning, to be a viable alternative to dictionary-based
methods? RQ2 is descriptive. The hypotheses were derived from the
literature and were not preregistered.

\subsection{Translation}\label{translation}

The EN→EN condition described below reproduces the
translate-then-analyze strategy of comparative text analysis
(\citeproc{ref-devriesNoLongerLost2018}{De Vries et al., 2018};
\citeproc{ref-lucasComputerAssistedTextAnalysis2015}{Lucas et al.,
2015}). For this condition, the French corpus was machine-translated
into English with Google Translate's document translation service. An
automated R workflow processed the documents in batches and preserved
document IDs, so that the French and English versions remain
aligned.\footnote{See \texttt{src/21\_translate\_to\_english.R} in the
  project repository.}

\subsection{Manual Annotation}\label{manual-annotation}

A validation benchmark of 200 sentences was randomly sampled from the
corpus. Each sentence was machine-translated into English with Google
Translate through the \texttt{polyglotR} package. This sample size falls
within the 100 to 300 units generally recommended for validating
automated sentiment measures against a manually coded gold standard
(\citeproc{ref-vanAtteveldt2021validity}{van Atteveldt et al., 2021}).
It yields 95\% confidence intervals of roughly ±0.05 around a
correlation of 0.80. Three independent coders rated the sentiment of
each sentence on a scale from -1 (very negative) to 1 (very positive),
using a custom Shiny application. The three ratings were averaged. This
average is the ground truth against which both dictionary and LLM
methods are evaluated.

Agreement between individual coders was moderate. Krippendorff's alpha
(interval) was 0.597 (95\% bootstrap CI {[}0.516, 0.656{]}). The mean
pairwise correlation between coders was 0.687. All three coders assigned
the same sign (negative, zero, or positive) to only 42.5\% of sentences.
Two features of the task account for much of this disagreement. Coders
used a fine-grained scale with more than 60 distinct values, and most
sentences carry mild sentiment, so small differences often cross zero.
The ground truth averages three coders and is therefore more reliable
than any single coder: the Spearman-Brown projection from the mean
pairwise correlation puts the reliability of the three-coder mean at
0.868. These levels still call for caution. Simulations show that low
intercoder reliability and small validation sets increase the risk of
misjudging automated methods
(\citeproc{ref-songValidationsWeTrust2020}{Song et al., 2020}). The
Limitations section returns to this point.

\subsection{Dictionary-Based Sentiment
Analysis}\label{dictionary-based-sentiment-analysis}

Lexicon-based scoring was applied to the French original and to the
English translation as a classical baseline. For French text, a
customized version of the French Lexicoder Sentiment Dictionary
(\texttt{frlsd}) (\citeproc{ref-duval_petry16}{Duval \& Pétry, 2016})
was applied to lowercased, stopword-removed, sentence-segmented text.
Each sentence was scored as (positive terms − negative terms) / total
words. For the English translation, the standard Lexicoder Sentiment
Dictionary (\citeproc{ref-young_andsoroka12}{Young \& Soroka, 2012}) was
used, with additional handling of contractions, proper nouns, and
negation (for example, ``not good'' scored as negative). Each sentence
was scored as ((positive + negated negative) − (negative + negated
positive)) / total words. The two dictionaries therefore differ in
construction as well as in language. This matters when they are
compared.

\subsection{LLM-Based Sentiment
Analysis}\label{llm-based-sentiment-analysis}

Fifteen general-purpose language models were evaluated. They were
selected for prominence and affordability, as realistic choices for
researchers who need to analyze large corpora. Proprietary models raise
reproducibility concerns, so their inclusion requires justification
(\citeproc{ref-palmerUsingProprietaryLanguage2023}{Palmer et al.,
2023}). Three closed-weight models from the major vendors serve as a
reference point: the studies that established LLM annotation in the
social sciences relied on proprietary GPT models
(\citeproc{ref-gilardiChatGPTOutperformsCrowd2023}{Gilardi et al.,
2023}; \citeproc{ref-heseltineLargeLanguageModels2024}{Heseltine \&
Clemm Von Hohenberg, 2024};
\citeproc{ref-rathjeGPTEffectiveTool2024}{Rathje et al., 2024};
\citeproc{ref-tornbergLargeLanguageModels2025}{T"ornberg, 2025}). Three
further models test specific claims. Mistral AI, a French developer,
describes its models as particularly strong in French. It also reports
that its Tekken tokenizer compresses French text about 30\% more
efficiently than its earlier tokenizer
(\citeproc{ref-mistralaiMistralNeMo2024}{Mistral AI, 2024}). Two Mistral
models test both claims. Jev tests a different kind of model, one that
returns a probability for each answer rather than generated text.

The ten open-weight models are Llama 3.2 1B, Llama 3.2 3B, Llama 3.1 8B,
GPT-OSS 20B, Mistral Small 3.2 24B, Qwen3 32B, Llama 4 Scout, GPT-OSS
120B, Qwen3 235B-A22B, and DeepSeek V3.2. They range from 1B to 671B
total parameters. The five closed-weight models are Claude Haiku 4.5
(Anthropic), Gemini 3.5 Flash (Google), GPT-5.6 Luna (OpenAI), Mistral
Large 4 (Mistral AI), and Jev 1.13 (TypeSafe). Mistral Large 4 was
evaluated in the preview release of October 2026. Mistral has announced
that its weights will be published, but they were not available at the
time of writing, so it is classified as closed-weight here. Jev is not a
text generator. It answers a typed question with a probability for each
possible answer. It received the same task as the other models, posed as
a question whose five answer levels are the five anchors of the
sentiment scale, worded as in the prompts below and in the language of
each condition. Its score is the probability-weighted average of the
five levels, mapped onto the -1 to 1 scale. This lineup updates an
initial 2024-2025 evaluation of 11 comparable models with an identical
design. Several models from that evaluation, including GPT-4o and Gemini
2.0 Flash, were deprecated by their providers within eighteen months.
The smallest model, Llama 3.2 1B, rarely followed the instruction to
answer with a number. About one call in 23 returned a parseable score.
Even after retries, it produced a usable score for only 58 to 74 of the
200 sentences, depending on the condition. It was retained deliberately.
Models of around 8B parameters can handle the task, whereas a 1B model
appears too small to follow the instructions reliably. This marks a
practical lower bound on model size. Its metrics rest on a reduced and
non-random subset of sentences and are not directly comparable to those
of the other models.

Each model was tested under three conditions: French prompts on French
text (FR→FR), English prompts on French text (EN→FR), and English
prompts on the machine-translated English text (EN→EN). The first two
conditions isolate the effect of prompt language. The last two isolate
the effect of text language.

All prompts followed the same structure: a task definition, a five-point
sentiment scale from -1.0 to 1.0 with anchor descriptions and
intermediate values allowed, an instruction to consider cultural and
linguistic nuance, and a constraint to respond with a numerical value
only. French prompts were direct translations of the English prompts.
The English-prompt/French-text version is shown below.\footnote{The
  French-prompt/French-text version is identical except for language.
  See \texttt{src/93\_prompts.R} in the project repository for the exact
  prompts, and \texttt{src/40\_prompt.R} for the full evaluation
  pipeline, including model initialization, retry logic, rate limiting,
  and checkpointing.}

The generative models were queried through the \texttt{ellmer} R
package. Claude Haiku 4.5, Gemini 3.5 Flash, and GPT-5.6 Luna were
accessed through their vendors' APIs. The ten open-weight models and
Mistral Large 4 were accessed through OpenRouter. Responses were capped
at 400 tokens. OpenRouter can serve the same model through several
providers at different numerical precisions. Each model accessed through
OpenRouter was therefore pinned to a single named provider endpoint, at
the highest precision offered, with fallback to other providers
disabled. Mistral Large 4 is served only by Mistral itself.\footnote{Endpoint
  pins, and the reasoning settings used to suppress chain-of-thought in
  models that reason by default, are listed in
  \texttt{src/94\_models\_map.R}. Mistral Large 4 reasons by default,
  exhausts the 400-token cap, and returns no answer. Its reasoning was
  disabled, as for Qwen3 32B and DeepSeek V3.2.} Gemini 3.5 Flash was
queried with its default settings. Under these settings it generates
reasoning tokens before answering: 354 output tokens per call on
average, against 7 for Claude Haiku 4.5. Jev was queried through
TypeSafe's API, with the model version pinned.\footnote{See
  \texttt{src/42\_jev\_prompt.R} and \texttt{src/98\_jev\_helpers.R}.}
Each sentence was scored three times per model and condition (27,000
scores in total), and the mean of the three runs was taken as the final
score. Despite explicit instructions to respond with a bare number, the
generative models frequently returned formatted text, such as
``Sentiment: 0.5''. These responses were parsed with a pattern-matching
function, and only values within the -1.0 to 1.0 range were retained.
Jev's answers needed no parsing.

To check whether the sentence-level findings hold at the scale of a real
corpus, the best-performing model on the benchmark, GPT-5.6 Luna, also
scored all 2,683 full articles under the FR→FR and EN→FR conditions,
with the same prompts and settings.\footnote{See
  \texttt{src/70\_prompt\_corpus.R}. The two passes required 5,366 API
  calls, 7.1 million input tokens and 187,000 output tokens, for USD
  1.64 at list prices.} These scores are compared with the French
Lexicoder dictionary in the Appendix (Figure 5).

\subsection{Validation and Performance
Evaluation}\label{validation-and-performance-evaluation}

Performance was assessed with four metrics: Pearson correlation between
each model's continuous predictions and the ground truth; mean absolute
error (MAE); weighted F1 on a 7-category classification, from ``very
negative'' to ``very positive''; and weighted F1 on a 3-category
classification (negative, neutral, positive) that groups the 7
categories into polarity bands. Weighted rather than macro F1 is used
because the manually coded categories are unevenly distributed.
``Somewhat positive'' alone accounts for 44\% of annotated sentences,
while ``very negative'' and ``very positive'' each account for 1\% or
less. Comparing the two classification schemes separates fine-grained
discrimination (7 categories) from polarity classification (3
categories). All metrics were calculated on complete cases only.

Condition comparisons are paired by model and map onto the hypotheses.
The prompt-language hypotheses compare FR→FR with EN→FR, which hold the
French text constant. The translation hypotheses compare EN→FR with
EN→EN, which hold the English prompt constant and isolate the effect of
analyzing the translation. The comparison of FR→FR with EN→EN contrasts
the two complete workflows a researcher might adopt. A non-significant
t-test cannot by itself establish the absence of an effect. Alongside
paired t-tests, two one-sided tests (TOST) therefore assess whether a
difference is small enough to be treated as negligible, with an
equivalence bound of ±0.05 on each metric's scale. These comparisons and
the averages reported in the Results cover the fourteen models that
scored all 200 sentences (42 model-condition combinations). They exclude
the dictionary baselines, which have no prompt. Llama 3.2 1B is reported
separately: it scored a different, partial subset of sentences in each
condition, so comparisons across conditions would compare unlike
samples.

\section{Results}\label{results}

\subsection{Prompt Language (H1a
vs.~H1b)}\label{prompt-language-h1a-vs.-h1b}

Holding the text constant, prompt language made no detectable
difference. French prompts on French text (FR→FR) scored slightly higher
than English prompts on the same text (EN→FR) on every metric: mean
correlation 0.708 vs.~0.687, MAE 0.208 vs.~0.216, 3-category F1 0.674
vs.~0.656, and 7-category F1 0.381 vs.~0.363. None of these paired
differences was statistically significant (all \emph{p} \textgreater{}
0.10). Equivalence tests go further. FR→FR and EN→FR were statistically
equivalent within ±0.05 on MAE (\emph{p} \textless{} 0.001), 7-category
F1 (\emph{p} = 0.015), and 3-category F1 (\emph{p} = 0.006). Correlation
was the one metric on which equivalence could not be established. The
90\% confidence interval of the difference, {[}−0.024, 0.066{]}, leaves
room for a French-prompt advantage of up to 0.066, but not for an
English-prompt advantage larger than 0.024. The results contradict H1a
and support H1b on three of four metrics.

\subsection{Machine Translation (H2a
vs.~H2b)}\label{machine-translation-h2a-vs.-h2b}

For the LLMs, translation carried a cost. Holding the English prompt
constant, analyzing the translation instead of the original (EN→EN
vs.~EN→FR) lowered 3-category F1 by 0.035 (0.621 vs.~0.656, \emph{p} =
0.004). The accompanying drop in correlation (0.659 vs.~0.687) was not
statistically significant (\emph{p} = 0.12). Differences in MAE and
7-category F1 remained within the ±0.05 equivalence bound. Compared with
the fully French workflow (FR→FR), the fully English workflow (EN→EN)
lowered correlation by 0.048 (\emph{p} = 0.002) and 3-category F1 by
0.053 (\emph{p} \textless{} 0.001).

The dictionaries showed the opposite pattern. The English Lexicoder
applied to the translation correlated with human judgments at r = 0.548,
against 0.357 for the French Lexicoder applied to the original. The
difference is 0.191 (95\% bootstrap CI {[}0.059, 0.326{]}). The English
dictionary also reached a higher 3-category F1 (0.588 vs.~0.557). The
two dictionaries also differ in construction, so this contrast does not
isolate language. Its direction nonetheless matches the bridge view. For
the LLMs, the results contradict H2a and support H2b for polarity
classification and, in the comparison of complete workflows, for
correlation.

\subsection{Viability Against Dictionary Methods
(RQ2)}\label{viability-against-dictionary-methods-rq2}

General-purpose LLMs, used without fine-tuning, correlated with human
judgments well above both dictionaries. The best configurations exceeded
r = 0.80. GPT-5.6 Luna led on correlation (r = 0.810 in FR→FR). Claude
Haiku 4.5 led on 3-category F1 (0.778) and 7-category F1 (0.537), both
in FR→FR. Gemini 3.5 Flash led on MAE (0.156 in EN→FR). Jev came close
behind the leaders. It reached r = 0.772 in both conditions on French
text and the second-highest 7-category F1 (0.528 in EN→FR). Twelve of
the 42 model-condition combinations correlated with the ground truth
more strongly than individual human coders agreed with the mean of the
other two (mean leave-one-out r = 0.748, range 0.724--0.779).\footnote{This
  comparison slightly favors the models, because the ground truth they
  are compared against averages three coders rather than two. Agreement
  between annotators is not a strict ceiling on machine performance
  (\citeproc{ref-richieInterannotatorAgreementNot2022}{Richie et al.,
  2022}).}

Closed-weight models outperformed open-weight ones on average (mean r =
0.754 vs.~0.646; 3-category F1 0.685 vs.~0.632). The gap closed at the
top of the open-weight range. Qwen3 235B-A22B reached r = 0.774 in
FR→FR, above Gemini 3.5 Flash in the same condition. GPT-OSS 120B and
GPT-OSS 20B averaged r = 0.733 and 0.716 across conditions. Mistral
Small 3.2 reached the highest 3-category F1 of any open-weight model
(0.721 in FR→FR), although its scores sat further from the human ratings
(MAE 0.263 to 0.294). Mistral's newest flagship did not lead its own
family. Mistral Large 4 scored below Mistral Small 3.2 on correlation (r
= 0.692 to 0.713) and 3-category F1 (0.609 to 0.624), though with a
lower MAE. Performance degraded at smaller sizes. Llama 3.1 8B remained
usable (r = 0.575--0.612). Llama 3.2 3B was erratic (r = 0.476 in FR→FR
but 0.132 in EN→FR). Llama 3.2 1B failed: it returned a usable score for
only 58 to 74 sentences per condition, and on those its correlation with
the ground truth ranged from −0.199 to 0.100. Cost was not a barrier. At
list prices, scoring 1,000 sentences cost between USD 0.02 and 0.40 for
every model but one (Table~\ref{tbl-cost}). The exception, Gemini 3.5
Flash (USD 3.77), reflects the reasoning tokens it generates by default
rather than its per-token price.\footnote{No cost is reported for Llama
  3.2 1B. Its endpoint returned the same input token count on every call
  regardless of the prompt, so its usage was not measured. It needed
  22.7 API calls per usable score, against 1.0 to 1.03 for every other
  model, and would in any case have been the most expensive open-weight
  model to run.}

Against the LLM mean of r = 0.685, the dictionaries trailed on
correlation (0.548 and 0.357) and on 3-category F1 (0.588 and 0.557
vs.~0.651). On correlation, the comparison is one-sided: every model
from 8B parameters upward exceeded both dictionaries in every condition,
and only Llama 3.2 3B and 1B fell below them. On 3-category F1, the
translated English dictionary outperformed Qwen3 32B in every condition,
GPT-OSS 120B in EN→EN, and the two smallest Llama models. On MAE, both
dictionaries (0.203 and 0.210) did slightly better than the LLM average
(0.211). This reflects the dictionaries' compressed output rather than
their accuracy. Their scores cluster near zero (SD = 0.065 for the
English dictionary, against 0.286 for the ground truth and 0.30 to 0.49
for the models from 8B parameters upward). On a sample dominated by
mildly positive sentences, this keeps absolute errors small while
discriminating poorly between sentences.

\subsection{Classification
Granularity}\label{classification-granularity}

All models performed substantially better on 3-category than on
7-category classification (best F1 0.778 vs.~0.537; mean 0.651
vs.~0.369; Figure 3). Models identified positive and negative sentiment
most reliably (7-category F1 = 0.480 and 0.454, rising to 0.706 and
0.626 in the 3-category scheme). They struggled most with the extreme
categories (very negative F1 = 0.260, very positive F1 = 0.135), which
are nearly absent from the sample (two sentences and one sentence).
Neutral remained the hardest 3-category label (F1 = 0.272).

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

\subsection{Prompt Language: Multilingual Competence, Within Scope
Conditions}\label{prompt-language-multilingual-competence-within-scope-conditions}

For French and current models, the results side with the
multilingual-competence view. English prompts brought no advantage, and
equivalence tests ruled out any English-prompt gain larger than a few
hundredths on every metric. Earlier social science evidence showed that
labels agree across prompt languages
(\citeproc{ref-rathjeGPTEffectiveTool2024}{Rathje et al., 2024}). This
study extends that finding to the accuracy of the labels against human
judgment. It is also consistent with the minimal differences observed
for the strongest model in Arabic
(\citeproc{ref-Kmainasi2025Native}{Kmainasi et al., 2025}). The results
do not refute the pivot-language evidence so much as delimit it. That
evidence comes largely from reasoning and inference benchmarks, older or
smaller models, and lower-resourced languages
(\citeproc{ref-laiChatGPTEnglishComprehensive2023}{Lai et al., 2023};
\citeproc{ref-Lin2022Few}{X. V. Lin et al., 2022};
\citeproc{ref-Muennighoff2023Crosslingual}{Muennighoff et al., 2023}).
The representational bias toward English that it points to
(\citeproc{ref-wendler_etal24}{Wendler et al., 2024}) need not produce
worse instruction-following for a simple judgment in a language as well
represented as French. The smaller open-weight models behaved
differently. Llama 3.2 3B correlated with human judgments at r = 0.48
with French prompts but 0.13 with English ones. This suggests that
sensitivity to prompt language may reappear below a certain model size,
although a single model cannot establish this. At the scale of the full
corpus, the equivalence of prompt languages is even clearer. GPT-5.6
Luna's scores for the 2,683 articles under French and English prompts
correlated at r = 0.97, and 94\% of articles received the same sign
(Figure 5).

Prompt language does carry a cost that the accuracy results do not show.
The French prompt is 6\% longer than the English one in characters, yet
it cost 10\% to 34\% more input tokens, depending on the model
(Table~\ref{tbl-tokens}).\footnote{Jev's input is TypeSafe's structured
  question rather than the prompt shown above, so its token counts do
  not measure the same text. It is left out of the tokenizer comparisons
  in this paragraph.} The difference is a property of each model's
tokenizer, not of its size or licence. Vocabularies that favor English
encode English text with similar efficiency across models (1.17 to 1.25
tokens per word). They split French words into more pieces, at rates
from 1.38 tokens per word (Mistral Large 4) to 1.87 (Claude Haiku 4.5).
Ahia et al. (\citeproc{ref-ahiaAllLanguagesCost2023}{2023}) document the
same disparity across many languages. Mistral's claim of a tokenizer
that is cheaper on French
(\citeproc{ref-mistralaiMistralNeMo2024}{Mistral AI, 2024}) held for one
of its two models. Mistral Large 4 encoded French more compactly than
any other model and had the smallest French-prompt overhead (10\%).
Mistral Small 3.2 did not stand out (1.45 tokens per French word, a 15\%
overhead). The French sentences themselves cost 1.6\% to 4.6\% more
tokens than their English translations. Output length did not differ by
prompt language. Since accuracy was equivalent, the English prompt is
the cheaper of two equally good options. The saving depends on where a
model's cost lies. It is 14\% to 31\% per 1,000 sentences for most
models billed mostly on input (Claude Haiku 4.5, Llama 3.x, Qwen3,
DeepSeek V3.2, Llama 4 Scout, Mistral Small 3.2). It is 10\% for Mistral
Large 4, whose French overhead is smallest. It is under 6\% for GPT-5.6
Luna, the GPT-OSS models, and Gemini 3.5 Flash, whose output tokens
weigh more. Translating the text first would save a few percent more, at
the accuracy cost reported above.

\subsection{Translation: A Bridge for Dictionaries, a Loss for
LLMs}\label{translation-a-bridge-for-dictionaries-a-loss-for-llms}

The translation results are the article's main contribution to the
debate. The same machine translation that improved the dictionary
lowered the LLMs' performance. This divergence reconciles the bridge and
loss views rather than choosing between them. Translation helps tools
that cannot read the source language, and it costs information for tools
that can. The dictionary result is consistent with a long line of
evidence that translating into English lets researchers use
better-validated English resources
(\citeproc{ref-araujoComparativeStudyMachine2020}{Araújo et al., 2020};
\citeproc{ref-devriesNoLongerLost2018}{De Vries et al., 2018};
\citeproc{ref-prokschMultilingualSentimentAnalysis2019}{Proksch et al.,
2019}). LLMs already read French. For them, translation offers no such
gain, and it exposes the analysis to the alterations of affective
meaning that translation introduces
(\citeproc{ref-Mohammad2016translation}{Mohammad et al., 2016}). The
finding runs against evidence that translating inputs into English
improves multilingual models
(\citeproc{ref-Etxaniz2024Multilingual}{Etxaniz et al., 2024}). That
evidence rests on reasoning and inference benchmarks whose non-English
versions were produced by translation. The sentences analyzed here were
written in French. The practical implication is direct: the translation
step that dictionary-era workflows made necessary should not be carried
into LLM-based sentiment analysis of French text.

\subsection{Viability: Optimism, With
Validation}\label{viability-optimism-with-validation}

On RQ2, the results side with the optimistic literature
(\citeproc{ref-gilardiChatGPTOutperformsCrowd2023}{Gilardi et al.,
2023}; \citeproc{ref-rathjeGPTEffectiveTool2024}{Rathje et al., 2024};
\citeproc{ref-tornbergLargeLanguageModels2025}{T"ornberg, 2025}) and
extend it in two directions. First, the advantage of LLMs over
dictionaries holds against a native-language dictionary as well as a
translated one. The multilingual evidence had so far made this
comparison only in English. Second, open-weight models were viable from
about 8B parameters, and the largest approached the best closed-weight
models. This is consistent with evidence from German
(\citeproc{ref-klahnDictionariesLLMsEvaluation2025}{Klähn et al.,
2025}). Below that size, performance became erratic, and a 1B-parameter
model could not follow the task. For researchers weighing the
reproducibility concerns raised by proprietary models
(\citeproc{ref-ollionDangersUsingProprietary2024}{Ollion et al., 2024};
\citeproc{ref-spirlingWhyOpensourceGenerative2023}{Spirling, 2023}),
this makes a credible open-weight option available at low cost. Qwen3
235B-A22B and GPT-OSS 120B reached 90--95\% of the best closed-weight
correlation for USD 0.02--0.03 per thousand sentences. Models from a
French developer showed no French advantage. Neither Mistral model did
notably better with French prompts (r = 0.702 vs.~0.713 for Mistral
Large 4 and 0.735 vs.~0.728 for Mistral Small 3.2, FR→FR vs.~EN→FR), and
both trailed the leading closed-weight models. A different kind of model
also proved viable. Jev, which returns a probability for each point of
the scale rather than generated text, came within 0.04 of the best
correlation for about USD 0.02 per thousand sentences. For French text,
the answer to RQ2 is therefore yes. A general-purpose LLM measures
sentiment better than the Lexicoder dictionary on every criterion that
rewards discrimination, and the cost of running one is no longer a
reason to prefer the dictionary.

The cautious literature is not dismissed. Performance varied widely
across models, which reinforces the need to validate each model on each
task (\citeproc{ref-pangakisAutomatedAnnotationGenerative2023}{Pangakis
et al., 2023}). Two further results qualify the picture. First, the
dictionaries' near-zero output gave them a slightly lower MAE than the
LLMs. MAE alone can thus reward a method that barely discriminates.
Second, all models performed markedly worse on 7-category classification
than on 3-category polarity. These two results are compatible once
ordering and calibration are distinguished. LLMs order sentences and
reproduce the spread of human ratings. Their correlation with the ground
truth (mean r = 0.685, up to 0.810) far exceeds that of the dictionaries
(0.548 and 0.357). From 8B parameters upward, their scores spread at
least as widely as the human ratings (SD 0.30 to 0.49 vs.~0.286),
whereas the dictionaries' scores cluster near zero (SD = 0.065 for the
English dictionary). What LLMs do poorly is place scores on the right
side of fixed category thresholds. They overstate extremes: 10.6\% of
LLM scores exceed ±0.66, against 1.5\% of ground-truth values. A
sentence rated 0.5 by humans and 0.8 by a model is correctly ordered but
misclassified. The ground truth contributes to the mismatch. As an
average of three coders, it is pulled toward the center, and only three
sentences fall in the two extreme categories. Gradations in sentiment
are one of the main arguments for dictionaries
(\citeproc{ref-vanderveenAdvantagesLexiconbasedSentiment2025}{Van Der
Veen \& Bleich, 2025}). In this corpus, a single off-the-shelf
dictionary captured them less well than the LLMs. The gap does not close
with aggregation. On the full corpus, the French dictionary's article
scores correlated with GPT-5.6 Luna's at r = 0.36, and their monthly
averages at r = 0.42 (Figure 5). What the dictionary retains is
transparency, since every score can be traced to the words that produced
it, and stability over time, since a dictionary is not deprecated. These
make it a useful baseline and robustness check alongside an LLM. They
are not a reason to prefer it as the primary measure.

\section{Limitations}\label{limitations}

Several limitations qualify these findings. The benchmark is modest in
size and reliability. Its 200 sentences fall within common
recommendations for validating sentiment measures
(\citeproc{ref-vanAtteveldt2021validity}{van Atteveldt et al., 2021}),
but agreement between individual coders was moderate (Krippendorff's
alpha = 0.597). Simulations show that such conditions raise the risk of
misjudging automated methods
(\citeproc{ref-songValidationsWeTrust2020}{Song et al., 2020}).
Averaging three coders yields a more reliable ground truth, but it also
pulls ratings toward the center. Only two sentences fall in the very
negative category and one in the very positive, so class-level F1 scores
for these categories are unstable. The findings concern French only. The
corpus is French-language news on a single domain, open-source software.
Neither the absence of a prompt-language effect nor the cost of
translation can be assumed to extend to other languages or genres. This
applies especially to languages less represented in training data, where
the pivot-language evidence is strongest.

Methodologically, the EN→EN condition relies on Google Translate, and
translation quality was deliberately not validated. The condition
reproduces what a researcher who does not read the source language can
do: translate automatically and analyze the result. Its lower
performance should be read as the cost of that workflow rather than of
translation as such. The dictionary comparison confounds language with
dictionary construction. A single standardized prompt was used across
the generative models for comparability. This may understate the
performance achievable with model-specific prompting. Jev received the
same scale in a different format, and its score is a
probability-weighted average. Such averages are pulled toward the middle
of the scale, which can lower MAE without improving discrimination, so
Jev's MAE should be read with care. Mistral Large 4 was evaluated as a
preview release, which may change before its general availability. The
models tested do not include fine-tuned or domain-specific classifiers,
which can rival general-purpose LLMs
(\citeproc{ref-mochtakParlasentMappingSentiment2025}{Mochtak et al.,
2025}). Costs are reported at list prices and count cached prompt tokens
at the full input rate, so they are upper bounds. Finally, this
evaluation is a snapshot. Several models from an earlier version of this
study were deprecated within eighteen months, and the specific rankings
should be expected to age quickly.

\section{Conclusion}\label{conclusion}

Researchers analyzing French text with LLMs can set aside both inherited
reflexes. The language of the prompt is a matter of convenience. English
instructions brought no advantage, and equivalence tests rule out any
substantial one. The language of the text is not a matter of
convenience. Translating into English, the step that English-bound tools
made necessary, improved the dictionary but lowered the LLMs'
performance. The comparative strategy that served the dictionary era
does not transfer to models that read the source language.

Two questions follow. The first is whether these results hold beyond
French, particularly for languages less represented in training data,
where the case for English prompts and translation is strongest. The
design ports directly to such tests: the same three conditions, a
manually annotated benchmark, and a different target language. The
second is whether the size threshold observed here, below which
open-weight models become erratic and prompt-sensitive, is a stable
feature of current models.

On the practical question of whether a researcher working on French text
is still better served by a dictionary, the answer here is no. A
general-purpose LLM validated on a manually annotated sample measures
sentiment better than the Lexicoder dictionary on every criterion but
transparency. Open-weight models from about 8B parameters do so for a
few cents per thousand sentences. The dictionary keeps two advantages,
transparency and stability over time. These make it a baseline and a
robustness check rather than the primary measure. Every model still
needs to be validated on the task at hand. For researchers working with
French text, the evidence settles three points: use a validated LLM,
prompt in whichever language is convenient, and analyze the text in the
language in which it was written.

{\centering \pandocbounded{\includegraphics[keepaspectratio]{../../results/graphs/corpus_time_series.png}}

}

\end{figure}%

Figure 5 shows monthly mean sentiment for the 2,683 articles, scored by
GPT-5.6 Luna with French prompts and with English prompts, and by the
French Lexicoder dictionary on the original text. Points are monthly
means and curves are LOESS smooths. The two prompt languages produce
nearly identical series (r = 0.97 across 322 months). The dictionary
agrees with the model only weakly (r = 0.42 across months, 0.36 across
articles). The smoothed curves look alike because none of the three
series shows a strong trend, not because the dictionary and the model
agree on individual months.
