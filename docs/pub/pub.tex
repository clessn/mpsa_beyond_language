\title{Language Doesn't Matter: Evaluating the Efficacy of Large
Language Models in Analyzing Sentiment in French Textual Data}


\shorttitle{Language Doesn't Matter}


\usepackage{etoolbox}








\authorsnames{Laurence-Olivier M. Foisy,Camille Pelletier,Étienne
Proulx,Yannick Dufresne}





\affiliation{
{Département de science politique, Université Laval}}




\leftheader{Foisy, Pelletier, Proulx and Dufresne}

\date{2026-09-16}


\abstract{Researchers who analyze non-English text with large language
models (LLMs) inherit two rules of thumb: prompt in English, because
these models learned mostly from English, and translate the text into
English, as dictionary-based methods long required. The evidence behind
both rules is contested. This article tests them on French. Twelve open-
and closed-weight LLMs scored 200 manually annotated French news
sentences under three conditions: French prompts on French text, English
prompts on French text, and English prompts on machine-translated text,
with the English and French Lexicoder dictionaries as benchmarks. Prompt
language made no meaningful difference, and equivalence tests rule out
any substantial English-prompt advantage. Translation cut both ways: it
improved the dictionary but lowered the LLMs' polarity classification
and, relative to the fully French workflow, their correlation with human
judgments. The best models reached r = 0.81 and outperformed both
dictionaries; open-weight models from about 8B parameters were viable,
whereas a 1B model could not follow the task. For LLM-based sentiment
analysis of French text, prompt language is a matter of convenience, and
the text is best analyzed in the original. }

\keywords{Sentiment Analysis, Large Language Models, Machine
Translation, Prompt Language, French Language, Open-Weight
Models, Computational Social Science}

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Computational text analysis in the social sciences grew up around
English. Its tools, from sentiment dictionaries to topic models, were
built for English text, a dominance that has been criticized for
depressing comparative research and excluding scholarly communities that
work in other languages
(\citeproc{ref-badenThreeGapsComputational2022}{Baden et al., 2022}).
Comparativists coped by translating: machine translation made it
possible to convert multilingual corpora into English and apply
English-language tools (\citeproc{ref-devriesNoLongerLost2018}{De Vries
et al., 2018};
\citeproc{ref-lucasComputerAssistedTextAnalysis2015}{Lucas et al.,
2015}). Large language models (LLMs) promise to loosen this constraint.
Used without task-specific training, they match or exceed crowd workers
and expert coders on common annotation tasks
(\citeproc{ref-gilardiChatGPTOutperformsCrowd2023}{Gilardi et al.,
2023}; \citeproc{ref-tornbergLargeLanguageModels2025}{T"ornberg, 2025}),
and they do so, with some loss, in languages other than English
(\citeproc{ref-heseltineLargeLanguageModels2024}{Heseltine \& Clemm Von
Hohenberg, 2024}; \citeproc{ref-rathjeGPTEffectiveTool2024}{Rathje et
al., 2024}).

Yet the way researchers use LLMs on non-English text often carries over
two reflexes from the English-centered era. The first is to prompt in
English, on the grounds that these models are trained mostly on English
(\citeproc{ref-touvron_etal23a}{Touvron et al., 2023}) and that English
instructions have been found to work better even for non-English inputs
(\citeproc{ref-laiChatGPTEnglishComprehensive2023}{Lai et al., 2023};
\citeproc{ref-Lin2022Few}{X. V. Lin et al., 2022}). The second is to
translate the text into English before analysis, a practice inherited
from dictionary-based and bag-of-words methods that now reappears as
``translate-test'' with LLMs
(\citeproc{ref-Etxaniz2024Multilingual}{Etxaniz et al., 2024}). Whether
either reflex helps, harms, or makes no difference is unsettled. The
evidence comes mostly from reasoning and classification benchmarks in
natural language processing, often with older models and lower-resourced
languages, and the social science evidence is thin.

This article tests both reflexes on a measurement task central to
political communication research: sentiment analysis
(\citeproc{ref-vanAtteveldt2021validity}{van Atteveldt et al., 2021};
\citeproc{ref-young_andsoroka12}{Young \& Soroka, 2012}). Sentiment is a
demanding test for translation, because affective meaning is precisely
what translation can alter
(\citeproc{ref-Mohammad2016translation}{Mohammad et al., 2016}). Using
200 manually annotated sentences from French news coverage, the study
compares twelve open- and closed-weight LLMs under three conditions ---
French prompts on French text, English prompts on French text, and
English prompts on machine-translated text --- and benchmark them
against the English and French versions of the Lexicoder Sentiment
Dictionary. Equivalence tests allow the absence of a prompt-language
effect to be assessed directly rather than inferred from
non-significance.

The results speak to both reflexes. Prompt language made no meaningful
difference. Translation, by contrast, cut both ways: it improved the
dictionary but hurt the LLMs. The translation strategy that served
English-bound tools does not carry over to models that can read the
source language. The article also documents which models are viable for
this task, including a practical lower bound on the size of open-weight
models, and what they cost.

\section{Literature Review}\label{literature-review}

\subsection{LLMs as Measurement Instruments in the Social
Sciences}\label{llms-as-measurement-instruments-in-the-social-sciences}

Zero-shot LLM annotation has moved quickly from curiosity to candidate
standard. ChatGPT outperformed crowd workers on relevance, stance,
topic, and frame detection
(\citeproc{ref-gilardiChatGPTOutperformsCrowd2023}{Gilardi et al.,
2023}); GPT-4 outperformed expert coders and supervised classifiers at
identifying the party of politicians from their social media posts
across eleven countries
(\citeproc{ref-tornbergLargeLanguageModels2025}{T"ornberg, 2025}); and
GPT-4 closely approximated expert coding of negativity and sentiment in
tweets from the United States, Chile, Germany, and Italy
(\citeproc{ref-heseltineLargeLanguageModels2024}{Heseltine \& Clemm Von
Hohenberg, 2024}). Such results have fed broad claims that generative AI
could transform computational social science
(\citeproc{ref-bailCanGenerativeAI2024}{Bail, 2024};
\citeproc{ref-ziemsCanLargeLanguage2024}{Ziems et al., 2024}), chiefly
by removing the need for the thousands of annotated examples that
supervised learning requires
(\citeproc{ref-chaeLargeLanguageModels2026}{Chae \& Davidson, 2026};
\citeproc{ref-laurerLessAnnotatingMore2024}{Laurer et al., 2024}).

A more cautious literature qualifies these claims on three fronts.
First, performance is uneven. Across 25 English benchmarks, zero-shot
LLMs did not outperform the best fine-tuned models on classification
tasks (\citeproc{ref-ziemsCanLargeLanguage2024}{Ziems et al., 2024}),
and across 27 annotation tasks their performance was ``highly contingent
on both the dataset and the type of annotation task''
(\citeproc{ref-pangakisAutomatedAnnotationGenerative2023}{Pangakis et
al., 2023}). Validation against human coding therefore remains
indispensable (\citeproc{ref-grimmer_stewart13}{Grimmer \& Stewart,
2013}; \citeproc{ref-linNavigatingRisksUsing2026}{H. Lin \& Zhang,
2026}), and it is itself only as informative as the human gold standard
is reliable (\citeproc{ref-songValidationsWeTrust2020}{Song et al.,
2020}). Second, established tools retain advantages. Dictionaries
generalize across domains and preserve gradations of sentiment
(\citeproc{ref-vanderveenAdvantagesLexiconbasedSentiment2025}{Van Der
Veen \& Bleich, 2025}), even though off-the-shelf dictionaries validate
poorly against human judgment
(\citeproc{ref-vanAtteveldt2021validity}{van Atteveldt et al., 2021})
and are outperformed by transformer-based models in German political and
cultural texts (\citeproc{ref-klahnDictionariesLLMsEvaluation2025}{Klähn
et al., 2025};
\citeproc{ref-widmannCreatingComparingDictionary2023}{Widmann \& Wich,
2023}); a domain-specific multilingual classifier can also match GPT-4o
at a fraction of the cost
(\citeproc{ref-mochtakParlasentMappingSentiment2025}{Mochtak et al.,
2025}). Third, reliance on proprietary models threatens reproducibility
(\citeproc{ref-ollionDangersUsingProprietary2024}{Ollion et al., 2024};
\citeproc{ref-spirlingWhyOpensourceGenerative2023}{Spirling, 2023}),
which has prompted calls to justify their use
(\citeproc{ref-palmerUsingProprietaryLanguage2023}{Palmer et al., 2023})
and evidence that open-weight models can be competitive alternatives
(\citeproc{ref-alizadehOpensourceLLMsText2025}{Alizadeh et al., 2025};
\citeproc{ref-klahnDictionariesLLMsEvaluation2025}{Klähn et al., 2025}).

These debates leave the question of language largely implicit. The
broadest evaluations rely on English benchmarks
(\citeproc{ref-ziemsCanLargeLanguage2024}{Ziems et al., 2024}); studies
that extend to other languages report somewhat lower performance outside
English (\citeproc{ref-heseltineLargeLanguageModels2024}{Heseltine \&
Clemm Von Hohenberg, 2024};
\citeproc{ref-tornbergLargeLanguageModels2025}{T"ornberg, 2025}) but do
not examine how researchers should interact with models in those
languages. Even the most extensive multilingual evaluation in the social
sciences, covering 12 languages, compared GPT with dictionary methods on
English headlines only (\citeproc{ref-rathjeGPTEffectiveTool2024}{Rathje
et al., 2024}). Two choices that researchers working in other languages
must make --- the language of the prompt and the language of the text
--- have been debated elsewhere.

\subsection{Prompting in English: Pivot Language or Multilingual
Competence?}\label{prompting-in-english-pivot-language-or-multilingual-competence}

The case for prompting in English starts from the observation that LLMs
are trained predominantly on English
(\citeproc{ref-touvron_etal23a}{Touvron et al., 2023}) and perform less
well in other languages, especially lower-resourced ones
(\citeproc{ref-ahujaMEGAMultilingualEvaluation2023}{Ahuja et al., 2023};
\citeproc{ref-laiChatGPTEnglishComprehensive2023}{Lai et al., 2023}). A
mechanistic account suggests why: in Llama-2 models, intermediate
representations pass through an abstract ``concept space'' that lies
closer to English than to other languages
(\citeproc{ref-wendler_etal24}{Wendler et al., 2024}). If models
effectively reason through English, instructions written in English
should be understood best. Several findings support this pivot-language
view. ChatGPT performed better with English prompts even when the task
and input were in other languages
(\citeproc{ref-laiChatGPTEnglishComprehensive2023}{Lai et al., 2023});
English templates remedied the unexpectedly low accuracy of prompts
handcrafted by native speakers (\citeproc{ref-Lin2022Few}{X. V. Lin et
al., 2022}); models fine-tuned on English prompts performed best with
English prompts even on non-English samples
(\citeproc{ref-Muennighoff2023Crosslingual}{Muennighoff et al., 2023});
asking models to restate a non-English request in English improved their
multilingual performance (\citeproc{ref-huangNotAllLanguages2023}{Huang
et al., 2023}); and English instructions outperformed Arabic ones on
average across eleven Arabic classification tasks
(\citeproc{ref-Kmainasi2025Native}{Kmainasi et al., 2025}).

A competing view emphasizes multilingual competence. Large models show
strong multilingual reasoning abilities even in underrepresented
languages (\citeproc{ref-shiLanguageModelsAre2022}{Shi et al., 2022}),
and for capable models the language of the prompt appears to matter
little. In the Arabic study just cited, differences across prompt
languages were minimal for the strongest model, GPT-4o
(\citeproc{ref-Kmainasi2025Native}{Kmainasi et al., 2025}); GPT-4
Turbo's labels for Indonesian tweets under English and Indonesian
prompts agreed almost perfectly
(\citeproc{ref-rathjeGPTEffectiveTool2024}{Rathje et al., 2024}); and
changing the prompt language alters the length and wording of generated
responses while their meaning remains largely stable
(\citeproc{ref-nguyen_etal26}{Nguyen et al., 2026}). The evidence on
both sides comes mostly from reasoning and classification benchmarks,
older models, and lower-resourced languages, and none of it tests
whether prompt language affects the accuracy of a graded sentiment
judgment in a well-resourced language. The two views imply competing
expectations:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{H1a (pivot language).} English prompts yield more accurate
sentiment measurement of French text than French prompts.

\textbf{H1b (multilingual competence).} English and French prompts yield
equivalent performance on French text.
\end{quote}

\subsection{Translating the Text: Bridge or
Loss?}\label{translating-the-text-bridge-or-loss}

The second reflex predates LLMs. Faced with multilingual corpora,
comparative researchers translated texts into English so that
English-language tools could be applied
(\citeproc{ref-lucasComputerAssistedTextAnalysis2015}{Lucas et al.,
2015}), and this bridge strategy has been validated repeatedly.
Machine-translated and human-translated texts yield highly similar
term-document matrices and topic models
(\citeproc{ref-devriesNoLongerLost2018}{De Vries et al., 2018}), results
are robust across translation services
(\citeproc{ref-reberOvercomingLanguageBarriers2019}{Reber, 2019}), and a
machine translation of the Lexicoder dictionary captured legislative
conflict across European parliaments
(\citeproc{ref-prokschMultilingualSentimentAnalysis2019}{Proksch et al.,
2019}). For sentiment specifically, translating non-English sentences
into English and applying English methods performed as well as
language-specific methods
(\citeproc{ref-araujoComparativeStudyMachine2020}{Araújo et al., 2020}),
and sentiment analysis of English translations of Arabic posts was
competitive with sentiment analysis of the originals
(\citeproc{ref-Mohammad2016translation}{Mohammad et al., 2016}). The
logic has carried into the LLM era: translating inputs into English
before inference improves the performance of multilingual models
(\citeproc{ref-Etxaniz2024Multilingual}{Etxaniz et al., 2024}), is often
hard to beat for lower-resourced languages written in non-Latin scripts
(\citeproc{ref-ahujaMEGAMultilingualEvaluation2023}{Ahuja et al.,
2023}), and appears in applied research
(\citeproc{ref-Venerito2024Large}{Venerito \& Iannone, 2024}).

The opposing view treats translation as a source of loss. Translation
alters sentiment: affective words disappear or are replaced by words of
opposite valence, ambiguous words are mistranslated, and culturally
specific expressions lose their meaning
(\citeproc{ref-Mohammad2016translation}{Mohammad et al., 2016}).
Approaches that avoid translation altogether, such as multilingual
dictionaries (\citeproc{ref-maierMachineTranslationVs2022}{Maier et al.,
2022}) and multilingual sentence embeddings
(\citeproc{ref-lichtCrossLingualClassificationPolitical2023}{Licht,
2023}), yield comparable or more reliable results in some settings.
Crucially, the case for translation was built largely on tools that
cannot read the source language, such as English dictionaries and
bag-of-words models. For LLMs, which can, the supporting evidence comes
from benchmarks whose non-English versions were themselves created by
translation --- a setting that, as the authors note, may favor
translation-based methods --- rather than from text written natively in
the source language (\citeproc{ref-Etxaniz2024Multilingual}{Etxaniz et
al., 2024}). Again, the two views imply competing expectations:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{H2a (translation as bridge).} Analyzing English machine
translations yields sentiment measurement at least as accurate as
analyzing the French original.

\textbf{H2b (translation as loss).} Analyzing English machine
translations yields less accurate sentiment measurement than analyzing
the French original.
\end{quote}

\subsection{Research Questions}\label{research-questions}

These debates motivate two research questions. \textbf{RQ1:} Does the
language used to interact with the model --- that of the prompt, or that
of the text through machine translation --- affect LLM sentiment
measurement of French text? H1a--b and H2a--b offer competing answers.
\textbf{RQ2:} Can general-purpose LLMs, open and
closed-weight,\footnote{While ``open source'' traditionally implies
  access aligned with FOSS principles, its usage within the LLM context
  often refers more specifically to the public release of model weights,
  a condition more accurately described as ``open weights.'' This paper
  uses ``open-weight'' throughout.} measure sentiment in French text
well enough, without fine-tuning, to be a viable alternative to
dictionary-based methods? RQ2 is descriptive. The hypotheses were
derived from the literature and were not preregistered.

\subsection{Translation}\label{translation}

To enable the EN→EN condition described below, which reproduces the
translate-then-analyze strategy of comparative text analysis
(\citeproc{ref-devriesNoLongerLost2018}{De Vries et al., 2018};
\citeproc{ref-lucasComputerAssistedTextAnalysis2015}{Lucas et al.,
2015}), the French corpus was machine-translated into English using
Google Translate's document translation service, processed in batches
via an automated R workflow that preserved document IDs to keep French
and English versions aligned in the final dataset.\footnote{See
  \texttt{src/21\_translate\_to\_english.R} in the project repository
  for the full translation pipeline.}

\subsection{Manual Annotation}\label{manual-annotation}

A validation benchmark of 200 sentences was randomly sampled from the
corpus, and each sentence was machine-translated into English via Google
Translate and the \texttt{polyglotR} package. This sample size falls
within the 100 to 300 units generally recommended for validating
automated sentiment measures against a manually coded gold standard
(\citeproc{ref-vanAtteveldt2021validity}{van Atteveldt et al., 2021}),
and yields 95\% confidence intervals of roughly ±0.05 around a
correlation of 0.80. Three independent coders rated each sentence's
sentiment on a scale from -1 (very negative) to 1 (very positive) using
a custom Shiny application with keyboard shortcuts and scale reminders,
and the three ratings were averaged to obtain the ground truth used to
evaluate both dictionary-based and LLM methods.

Agreement between individual coders was moderate. Krippendorff's alpha
(interval) was 0.597 (95\% bootstrap CI {[}0.516, 0.656{]}), the mean
pairwise correlation between coders was 0.687, and all three coders
assigned the same sign (negative, zero, or positive) to only 42.5\% of
sentences. Two features of the task account for much of this: coders
used a fine-grained scale (more than 60 distinct values), and most
sentences carry mild sentiment, so small differences often cross zero.
Because the ground truth averages three coders, it is considerably more
reliable than any single coder: the Spearman-Brown projection from the
mean pairwise correlation puts the reliability of the three-coder mean
at 0.868. These levels nonetheless call for caution: simulations show
that low intercoder reliability and small validation sets increase the
risk of misjudging the performance of automated methods
(\citeproc{ref-songValidationsWeTrust2020}{Song et al., 2020}), a point
taken up in the Limitations.

\subsection{Dictionary-Based Sentiment
Analysis}\label{dictionary-based-sentiment-analysis}

Lexicon-based sentiment scoring was applied to both the French original
and the English translation as a classical baseline. For French text, a
customized version of the French Lexicoder Sentiment Dictionary
(\texttt{frlsd}) (\citeproc{ref-duval_petry16}{Duval \& Pétry, 2016})
was applied to lowercased, stopword-removed, sentence-segmented text,
scoring each sentence as (positive terms − negative terms) / total
words. For English translations, the standard Lexicoder Sentiment
Dictionary (\citeproc{ref-young_andsoroka12}{Young \& Soroka, 2012}) was
used with additional handling for contractions, proper nouns, and
negation (e.g., ``not good'' scored as negative), scoring each sentence
as ((positive + negated\_negative) − (negative + negated\_positive)) /
total words. The two dictionaries therefore differ in construction as
well as in language, which matters when they are compared.

\subsection{LLM-Based Sentiment
Analysis}\label{llm-based-sentiment-analysis}

Twelve general-purpose foundational LLMs were evaluated, selected for
prominence and affordability --- realistic choices for researchers who
need to analyze large corpora rather than a single sample. Given the
reproducibility concerns raised by proprietary models, their inclusion
requires justification
(\citeproc{ref-palmerUsingProprietaryLanguage2023}{Palmer et al.,
2023}): the three closed-weight models serve as a reference point,
because the studies that established LLM annotation in the social
sciences relied on proprietary GPT models
(\citeproc{ref-gilardiChatGPTOutperformsCrowd2023}{Gilardi et al.,
2023}; \citeproc{ref-heseltineLargeLanguageModels2024}{Heseltine \&
Clemm Von Hohenberg, 2024};
\citeproc{ref-rathjeGPTEffectiveTool2024}{Rathje et al., 2024};
\citeproc{ref-tornbergLargeLanguageModels2025}{T"ornberg, 2025}).

The evaluation spans nine open-weight models --- Llama 3.2 1B, Llama 3.2
3B, Llama 3.1 8B, GPT-OSS 20B, Qwen3 32B, Llama 4 Scout, GPT-OSS 120B,
Qwen3 235B-A22B, and DeepSeek V3.2, ranging from 1B to 671B total
parameters --- and three closed-weight models: Claude Haiku 4.5
(Anthropic), Gemini 3.5 Flash (Google), and GPT-5.6 Luna (OpenAI). This
lineup updates an initial 2024-2025 evaluation of 11 comparable models
using an identical experimental design; several models from that
original evaluation (e.g., GPT-4o, Gemini 2.0 Flash) had already been
deprecated by their providers within eighteen months, underscoring how
quickly this landscape moves. The smallest model, Llama 3.2 1B, rarely
followed the instruction to answer with a number: about one call in 23
returned a parseable score, and even after retries it produced a usable
score for only 58 to 74 of the 200 sentences, depending on the
condition. It was retained deliberately: while models of around 8B
parameters can handle the task, a 1B-parameter model appears too small
to follow the instructions reliably at all, which marks a practical
lower bound on model size. Its metrics rest on a reduced and non-random
subset of sentences and are not directly comparable to those of the
other models.

Each model was tested under three conditions to isolate the effects of
prompt language and text language: French prompts on French text
(FR→FR), English prompts on French text (EN→FR), and English prompts on
the machine-translated English text (EN→EN).

All prompts followed a standardized structure: a task definition, a
five-point sentiment scale from -1.0 to 1.0 with anchor descriptions
(allowing intermediate values), an instruction to consider cultural and
linguistic nuance, and a constraint to respond with only a numerical
value. French prompts were direct translations of the English prompts.

Each model was queried via the \texttt{ellmer} R package, using the
vendors' own APIs for the three closed-weight models and OpenRouter for
the nine open-weight models, with responses capped at 400 tokens.
Because OpenRouter can serve the same model through several providers at
different numerical precisions, each open-weight model was pinned to a
single named provider endpoint, at the highest precision offered, with
fallback to other providers disabled.\footnote{Endpoint pins, and the
  reasoning settings used to suppress chain-of-thought in models that
  reason by default, are listed in \texttt{src/94\_models\_map.R}.}
Gemini 3.5 Flash was queried with its default settings, under which it
generates reasoning tokens before answering (354 output tokens per call
on average, against 7 for Claude Haiku 4.5). Each sentence was scored
three times per model-condition combination (21,600 scores in total),
and the mean of the three runs was taken as the final score. Despite
explicit instructions to respond with a bare number, models frequently
returned formatted text (e.g., ``Sentiment: 0.5''); these responses were
parsed with a pattern-matching function validated against the expected
-1.0 to 1.0 range.

\subsection{Validation and Performance
Evaluation}\label{validation-and-performance-evaluation}

Performance was assessed with five complementary metrics: Pearson
correlation between each model's continuous predictions and the manually
annotated ground truth; Lin's concordance correlation coefficient (CCC),
which, unlike Pearson correlation, also penalizes differences in scale
and location, and so captures whether predictions reproduce the
intensity of human ratings rather than only their ordering; mean
absolute error (MAE); weighted F1 score on a 7-category classification
(``very\_negative'' through ``very\_positive''); and weighted F1 score
on a simplified 3-category classification (negative/neutral/positive)
that groups the 7 categories into broad polarity bands. Weighted (rather
than macro) F1 is used because the manually coded categories are
unevenly distributed --- ``somewhat\_positive'' alone accounts for 44\%
of annotated sentences, while ``very\_negative'' and ``very\_positive''
each account for 1\% or less --- so a weighted average better reflects
overall classification quality given this class imbalance. Comparing the
two classification schemes separates fine-grained sentiment
discrimination (7-category) from broad polarity classification
(3-category). All metrics were calculated on complete cases only.

Condition comparisons are paired by model and map onto the hypotheses.
The prompt-language hypotheses compare FR→FR with EN→FR, which hold the
French text constant. The translation hypotheses compare EN→FR with
EN→EN, which hold the English prompt constant and isolate the effect of
analyzing the translation; the comparison of FR→FR with EN→EN contrasts
the two complete workflows a researcher might adopt. Alongside paired
t-tests, two one-sided tests (TOST) against an equivalence bound of
±0.05 on each metric's scale assess whether a difference is small enough
to be treated as negligible, since a non-significant t-test cannot on
its own establish the absence of an effect. These comparisons and the
averages reported in the Results cover the eleven models that scored all
200 sentences (33 model-condition combinations) and exclude the
dictionary baselines, which have no prompt. Llama 3.2 1B is reported
separately: it scored a different, partial subset of sentences in each
condition, so its differences across conditions would compare unlike
samples.

\section{Results}\label{results}

\subsection{Prompt Language (H1a
vs.~H1b)}\label{prompt-language-h1a-vs.-h1b}

Holding the text constant, prompt language made no detectable
difference. French prompts on French text (FR→FR) edged out English
prompts on the same text (EN→FR) on every metric --- mean correlation
0.700 vs.~0.673, MAE 0.209 vs.~0.217, 3-category F1 0.669 vs.~0.646,
7-category F1 0.364 vs.~0.347 --- but none of these paired differences
was statistically significant (all \emph{p} \textgreater{} 0.12).
Equivalence tests go further: FR→FR and EN→FR were statistically
equivalent within ±0.05 on MAE (\emph{p} = 0.002), 7-category F1
(\emph{p} = 0.034), and 3-category F1 (\emph{p} = 0.034). Correlation
was the one metric where equivalence could not be established: the 90\%
confidence interval of the difference, {[}−0.032, 0.085{]}, leaves room
for a French-prompt advantage of up to 0.085, but not for an
English-prompt advantage larger than 0.032. The results thus contradict
H1a and support H1b on three of four metrics.

\subsection{Machine Translation (H2a
vs.~H2b)}\label{machine-translation-h2a-vs.-h2b}

For the LLMs, translation carried a cost. Holding the English prompt
constant, analyzing the translation instead of the original (EN→EN
vs.~EN→FR) lowered 3-category F1 by 0.04 (0.646 vs.~0.606, \emph{p} =
0.005); the accompanying drop in correlation (0.673 vs.~0.647) was not
statistically significant (\emph{p} = 0.24), and differences in MAE and
7-category F1 remained within the ±0.05 equivalence bound. Compared with
the fully French workflow (FR→FR), the fully English workflow (EN→EN)
lowered both correlation, by 0.053 (\emph{p} = 0.007), and 3-category
F1, by 0.063 (\emph{p} \textless{} 0.001).

The dictionaries showed the opposite pattern. The English Lexicoder
applied to the translation correlated with human judgments at r = 0.548,
against 0.357 for the French Lexicoder applied to the original --- a
difference of 0.191 (95\% bootstrap CI {[}0.059, 0.326{]}) --- and also
reached a higher 3-category F1 (0.588 vs.~0.557). Because the two
dictionaries also differ in construction, this contrast does not isolate
language, but its direction matches the bridge view. For the LLMs, the
results contradict H2a and support H2b for polarity classification and,
in the comparison of complete workflows, for correlation.

\subsection{Viability Against Dictionary Methods
(RQ2)}\label{viability-against-dictionary-methods-rq2}

General-purpose LLMs, used without fine-tuning, correlated with human
judgments well above both dictionaries. The best configurations exceeded
r = 0.80: GPT-5.6 Luna led on correlation (r = 0.810 in FR→FR) and
concordance (CCC = 0.779), Claude Haiku 4.5 on both 3-category F1
(0.778) and 7-category F1 (0.537, both FR→FR), and Gemini 3.5 Flash on
MAE (0.156 in EN→FR). Nine of the 33 model-condition combinations
correlated with the ground truth more strongly than individual human
coders agreed with the mean of the other two (mean leave-one-out r =
0.748, range 0.724--0.779).\footnote{This comparison favors the models
  slightly, since the ground truth they are compared against averages
  three coders rather than two. Agreement between annotators is not a
  strict ceiling on machine performance
  (\citeproc{ref-richieInterannotatorAgreementNot2022}{Richie et al.,
  2022}).}

Closed-weight models outperformed open-weight ones on average (mean r =
0.768 vs.~0.638; 3-category F1 0.689 vs.~0.622), but the gap closed at
the top of the open-weight range: Qwen3 235B-A22B reached r = 0.774 in
FR→FR, above Gemini 3.5 Flash in the same condition, and GPT-OSS 120B
and GPT-OSS 20B averaged r = 0.733 and 0.716 across conditions.
Performance degraded below that. Llama 3.1 8B remained usable (r =
0.575--0.612), Llama 3.2 3B was erratic (r = 0.476 in FR→FR but 0.132 in
EN→FR), and Llama 3.2 1B failed outright: it returned a usable score for
only 58 to 74 sentences per condition, and on those its correlation with
the ground truth ranged from −0.199 to 0.100. Cost was not a barrier: at
list prices, scoring 1,000 sentences cost between USD 0.02 and 0.40 for
every model but one (Table~\ref{tbl-cost}); the exception, Gemini 3.5
Flash (USD 3.77), reflects the reasoning tokens it generates by default
rather than its per-token price.

Against the LLM mean of r = 0.673, the dictionaries trailed on
correlation (0.548 and 0.357) and on 3-category F1 (0.588 and 0.557
vs.~0.640), though the English dictionary outperformed Llama 3.2 3B and
Qwen3 32B on the latter. The gap was widest on concordance: the LLMs
averaged a CCC of 0.631, against 0.233 and 0.181 for the English and
French dictionaries. On MAE, however, both dictionaries (0.203 and
0.210) did slightly better than the LLM average (0.213). This reflects
the dictionaries' compressed output rather than accuracy: their scores
cluster near zero (SD = 0.065 for the English dictionary, vs.~0.286 for
the ground truth and 0.38--0.47 for the LLMs), which keeps absolute
errors small on a sample dominated by mildly positive sentences while
discriminating poorly between them.

\subsection{Classification
Granularity}\label{classification-granularity}

All models performed substantially better on 3-category than 7-category
classification (best F1 of 0.778 vs.~0.537; mean 0.640 vs.~0.349; see
Figure 3, below). Models most reliably identified positive and negative
sentiment (7-category F1 = 0.482 and 0.441, rising to 0.690 and 0.627 in
the 3-category scheme) and struggled most with the extreme categories
(very\_negative F1 = 0.256, very\_positive F1 = 0.124), which are nearly
absent from the sample (two sentences and one sentence respectively).
Neutral remained the hardest 3-category label (F1 = 0.258).

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

\subsection{Prompt Language: Multilingual Competence, Within Scope
Conditions}\label{prompt-language-multilingual-competence-within-scope-conditions}

For French and current models, the results side with the
multilingual-competence view. English prompts brought no advantage, and
equivalence tests ruled out any English-prompt gain larger than a few
hundredths on every metric. This extends earlier social science
evidence, which showed that labels agree across prompt languages
(\citeproc{ref-rathjeGPTEffectiveTool2024}{Rathje et al., 2024}), to the
accuracy of those labels against human judgment, and it is consistent
with the minimal differences observed for the strongest model in Arabic
(\citeproc{ref-Kmainasi2025Native}{Kmainasi et al., 2025}). It does not
refute the pivot-language evidence so much as delimit it. That evidence
comes largely from reasoning and inference benchmarks, older or smaller
models, and lower-resourced languages
(\citeproc{ref-laiChatGPTEnglishComprehensive2023}{Lai et al., 2023};
\citeproc{ref-Lin2022Few}{X. V. Lin et al., 2022};
\citeproc{ref-Muennighoff2023Crosslingual}{Muennighoff et al., 2023}),
and the representational bias toward English it points to
(\citeproc{ref-wendler_etal24}{Wendler et al., 2024}) need not translate
into worse instruction-following for a simple judgment in a language as
well represented as French. The erratic behavior of the smaller
open-weight models in this study --- Llama 3.2 3B correlated with human
judgments at r = 0.48 with French prompts but 0.13 with English ones ---
hints that sensitivity to prompt language may reappear below a certain
model size, though a single model cannot establish this.

\subsection{Translation: A Bridge for Dictionaries, a Loss for
LLMs}\label{translation-a-bridge-for-dictionaries-a-loss-for-llms}

The translation results are the article's main contribution to the
debate. The same machine translation that improved the dictionary
lowered the LLMs' performance. This divergence reconciles the bridge and
loss views rather than choosing between them: translation helps tools
that cannot read the source language, and costs information for tools
that can. The dictionary result is consistent with a long line of
evidence that translating into English lets researchers use
better-validated English resources
(\citeproc{ref-araujoComparativeStudyMachine2020}{Araújo et al., 2020};
\citeproc{ref-devriesNoLongerLost2018}{De Vries et al., 2018};
\citeproc{ref-prokschMultilingualSentimentAnalysis2019}{Proksch et al.,
2019}). For LLMs, which already read French, translation offers no such
gain and exposes the analysis to the alterations of affective meaning
that translation introduces
(\citeproc{ref-Mohammad2016translation}{Mohammad et al., 2016}). The
finding runs against evidence that translating inputs into English
improves multilingual models
(\citeproc{ref-Etxaniz2024Multilingual}{Etxaniz et al., 2024}), but that
evidence rests on reasoning and inference benchmarks whose non-English
versions were produced by translation, whereas the sentences analyzed
here were written in French. The practical implication is direct: the
translation step that dictionary-era workflows made necessary should not
be carried into LLM-based sentiment analysis of French text.

\subsection{Viability: Optimism, With
Validation}\label{viability-optimism-with-validation}

On RQ2, the results side with the optimistic literature
(\citeproc{ref-gilardiChatGPTOutperformsCrowd2023}{Gilardi et al.,
2023}; \citeproc{ref-rathjeGPTEffectiveTool2024}{Rathje et al., 2024};
\citeproc{ref-tornbergLargeLanguageModels2025}{T"ornberg, 2025}) and
extend it in two directions. First, the advantage of LLMs over
dictionaries holds against a native-language dictionary as well as a
translated one, a comparison the multilingual evidence had so far made
only in English. Second, open-weight models were viable from about 8B
parameters, and the largest approached the best closed-weight models,
consistent with evidence from German
(\citeproc{ref-klahnDictionariesLLMsEvaluation2025}{Klähn et al.,
2025}); below that size, performance became erratic, and a 1B-parameter
model could not follow the task. For researchers weighing the
reproducibility concerns raised by proprietary models
(\citeproc{ref-ollionDangersUsingProprietary2024}{Ollion et al., 2024};
\citeproc{ref-spirlingWhyOpensourceGenerative2023}{Spirling, 2023}),
this makes a credible open-weight option available at low cost.

The cautious literature is not thereby dismissed. Performance varied
widely across models, which reinforces the need to validate each model
on each task
(\citeproc{ref-pangakisAutomatedAnnotationGenerative2023}{Pangakis et
al., 2023}). Two further results qualify the picture. First, the
dictionaries' near-zero output gave them a slightly lower MAE than the
LLMs --- a reminder that MAE alone can reward a method that barely
discriminates. Second, all models performed markedly worse on
fine-grained 7-category classification than on 3-category polarity.
These two results are compatible once ordering and calibration are
distinguished. LLMs capture sentiment intensity in the sense of ordering
sentences and reproducing the spread of human ratings: their concordance
with the ground truth (mean CCC = 0.631, up to 0.779) far exceeds that
of the dictionaries (0.233 and 0.181). What they do poorly is place
scores on the right side of fixed category thresholds. They overstate
extremes --- 10.8\% of LLM scores exceed ±0.66, against 1.5\% of
ground-truth values --- so a sentence rated 0.5 by humans and 0.8 by a
model is correctly ordered but misclassified. The ground truth
contributes to the mismatch: as an average of three coders, it is pulled
toward the center, leaving only three sentences in the two extreme
categories. Gradations in sentiment are one of the main arguments made
for dictionaries
(\citeproc{ref-vanderveenAdvantagesLexiconbasedSentiment2025}{Van Der
Veen \& Bleich, 2025}); in this corpus, a single off-the-shelf
dictionary captured them less well than the LLMs, though its
transparency remains an advantage no model offers.

\section{Limitations}\label{limitations}

Several limitations qualify these findings. The benchmark is modest in
size and reliability. Its 200 sentences fall within common
recommendations for validating sentiment measures
(\citeproc{ref-vanAtteveldt2021validity}{van Atteveldt et al., 2021}),
but agreement between individual coders was moderate (Krippendorff's
alpha = 0.597), and simulations show that such conditions raise the risk
of misjudging automated methods
(\citeproc{ref-songValidationsWeTrust2020}{Song et al., 2020}).
Averaging three coders yields a more reliable ground truth, but it also
pulls ratings toward the center, and only two sentences fall in the very
negative category and one in the very positive, so class-level F1 scores
for these categories are unstable. The findings concern French only: the
corpus is French-language news on a single technical and political
domain (open-source software), so neither the absence of a
prompt-language effect nor the cost of translation can be assumed to
extend to other languages, especially those less represented in training
data, where the pivot-language evidence is strongest, or to other
genres.

Methodologically, the EN→EN condition relies on Google Translate, and
translation quality was deliberately not validated: the condition
reproduces what a researcher who does not read English can do, namely
translate automatically and analyze the result. Its lower performance
should accordingly be read as the cost of that workflow rather than of
translation as such. The dictionary comparison confounds language with
dictionary construction. A single standardized prompt was used across
models for comparability, which may understate the performance
achievable with model-specific prompting, and the models tested do not
include fine-tuned or domain-specific classifiers, which can rival
general-purpose LLMs
(\citeproc{ref-mochtakParlasentMappingSentiment2025}{Mochtak et al.,
2025}). Costs are reported at list prices and count cached prompt tokens
at the full input rate, so they are upper bounds. Finally, this
evaluation is a snapshot: several models from an earlier version of this
study were deprecated within eighteen months, and the specific rankings
should be expected to age quickly.

\section{Conclusion}\label{conclusion}

Researchers analyzing French text with LLMs can set aside both inherited
reflexes. The language of the prompt is a matter of convenience: English
instructions brought no advantage, and equivalence tests rule out any
substantial one. The language of the text is not: translating into
English, the step that English-bound tools made necessary, improved the
dictionary but lowered the LLMs' performance. The comparative strategy
that served the dictionary era does not transfer to models that read the
source language.

Two questions follow. The first is whether these results hold beyond
French, particularly for languages less represented in training data,
where the case for English prompts and translation is strongest; the
design ports directly to such tests --- the same three conditions, a
manually annotated benchmark, a different target language. The second is
whether the size threshold observed here, below which open-weight models
become erratic and prompt-sensitive, is a stable feature of current
models. Method choice remains a judgment about research goals rather
than a ranking: dictionaries retain a transparency no model offers, and
every model still needs to be validated on the task at hand. What the
evidence here settles is narrower and, for researchers working with
French text, useful: prompt in whichever language is convenient, and
analyze the text in the language in which it was written.
