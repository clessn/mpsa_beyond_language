\title{Language Doesn't Matter: Evaluating the Efficacy of Large
Language Models in Analyzing Sentiment in French Textual Data}


\shorttitle{Language Doesn't Matter}


\usepackage{etoolbox}








\authorsnames{Laurence-Olivier M. Foisy,Camille Pelletier,Étienne
Proulx,Yannick Dufresne}





\affiliation{
{Département de science politique, Université Laval}}




\leftheader{Foisy, Pelletier, Proulx and Dufresne}

\date{2026-09-16}


\abstract{This study examines whether general-purpose large language
models (LLMs) can analyze sentiment in French text without task-specific
fine-tuning, and whether prompt language affects their performance.
Twelve open- and closed-weight LLMs are evaluated on 200 French
sentences drawn from a corpus of 2,683 French news articles on
open-source software, and compared with dictionary-based methods across
three conditions: French prompts on French text, English prompts on
French text, and English prompts on machine-translated English text.
Prompt language has no statistically meaningful effect on performance,
and equivalence tests rule out any substantial advantage of English
prompts, contrary to the English-prompting advantage reported in prior
work; machine-translating the text into English, by contrast, lowers
performance. Leading closed-weight models achieve strong performance (r
\textgreater{} 0.80), larger open-weight models offer a viable,
lower-cost alternative, and the smallest model tested (1B parameters)
fails to follow the task instructions. LLMs outperform a standard
sentiment dictionary on correlation with human judgments and on coarse
classification, though the dictionary offers greater transparency. All
models perform substantially better on three-category
(positive/neutral/negative) than on seven-category classification. These
findings suggest social science researchers can use general-purpose LLMs
for sentiment analysis of French text with either French or English
prompts, without sacrificing accuracy, but should analyze the original
text rather than an English translation. }

\keywords{Sentiment Analysis, Large Language models, Cross-lingual
NLP, French Language, Open-Source models, Computational Social
Science, Prompt Engineering}

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Most foundational large language models (LLMs) are trained predominantly
on English data (\citeproc{ref-touvron_etal23a}{Touvron et al., 2023}),
and a growing body of research reports that prompting in English
improves performance on non-English tasks, even when the target text
itself is non-English (\citeproc{ref-Jiang2020How}{Jiang et al., 2020};
\citeproc{ref-Lin2022Few}{Lin et al., 2022};
\citeproc{ref-Muennighoff2023Crosslingual}{Muennighoff et al., 2023};
\citeproc{ref-Nie2024Decomposed}{Nie et al., 2024}). If this ``English
prompt advantage'' holds generally, it imposes a real cost on
non-anglophone researchers, who would need to translate their prompts to
get the best results from otherwise capable models.

This paper puts that premise to the test in the case of French.
Sentiment analysis of French-language text offers a demanding case:
sentiment carries cultural and linguistic nuance that machine
translation can alter (\citeproc{ref-Mohammad2016translation}{Mohammad
et al., 2016}); it is a common measurement task in political
communication and the social sciences more broadly
(\citeproc{ref-vanAtteveldt2021validity}{van Atteveldt et al., 2021};
\citeproc{ref-young_andsoroka12}{Young \& Soroka, 2012}); it admits both
continuous (intensity) and categorical (polarity) evaluation
(\citeproc{ref-young_andsoroka12}{Young \& Soroka, 2012}); and
established dictionary-based methods, including a French adaptation of
the Lexicoder Sentiment Dictionary, offer a meaningful performance
benchmark (\citeproc{ref-duval_petry16}{Duval \& Pétry, 2016};
\citeproc{ref-vanAtteveldt2021validity}{van Atteveldt et al., 2021};
\citeproc{ref-young_andsoroka12}{Young \& Soroka, 2012}). Although
limited to a single language, the case bears on the broader premise that
prompting in English is the safer choice whatever the language of the
text.

Testing this premise presupposes that general-purpose LLMs --- used off
the shelf, without task-specific fine-tuning --- can analyze sentiment
in a French-language corpus competently in the first place, whether
prompted in French or in English. Specialized models such as BERT
variants achieve strong performance through fine-tuning
(\citeproc{ref-Anirban2021Sentiment}{Anirban \& Abhik, 2021};
\citeproc{ref-Michailidis2024Comparative}{Michailidis, 2024};
\citeproc{ref-Rafeef2023Evaluation}{Rafeef A. Hameed et al., 2023}), but
require machine learning expertise, computational resources, and labeled
data that many social science researchers lack
(\citeproc{ref-Chalkidis2020LEGAL}{Chalkidis et al., 2020};
\citeproc{ref-Laurer2023Less}{Laurer et al., 2023};
\citeproc{ref-Strubell2019Energy}{Strubell et al., 2019}).
General-purpose LLMs accessed through APIs offer a more accessible
alternative, requiring primarily prompting skills
(\citeproc{ref-Ghosh2023Navigating}{Ghosh et al., 2023};
\citeproc{ref-Perron2024Demystifying}{Perron et al., 2024}) --- but only
if they perform adequately without fine-tuning in the first place.

This study therefore asks two questions: (1) does prompt language
(French vs.~English) meaningfully affect sentiment analysis performance
on French text, and (2) can general-purpose LLMs --- open and
closed-weight\footnote{While ``open source'' traditionally implies
  access aligned with FOSS principles, its usage within the LLM context
  often refers more specifically to the public release of model weights,
  a condition more accurately described as ``open weights.'' This paper
  adopts this common LLM terminology while acknowledging that most
  models in the ``open source'' category are technically ``open
  weights.''} --- perform this task well enough, without fine-tuning, to
be a viable alternative to dictionary-based methods for social science
research? For the first question, the literature above implies a
directional expectation, derived from prior work rather than
preregistered: \emph{H1: English prompts outperform French prompts on
French text.} The second question is descriptive and carries no
hypothesis. The results do not support H1, and several models ---
including open-weight ones --- outperform a traditional dictionary
method.

\section{Literature Review}\label{literature-review}

Specialized, fine-tuned models achieve strong multilingual sentiment
analysis performance --- often above 85-95\% accuracy across
Indo-European, Semitic, and Asian languages
(\citeproc{ref-Ahmadian2024Hybrid}{Ahmadian et al., 2024};
\citeproc{ref-Anirban2021Sentiment}{Anirban \& Abhik, 2021};
\citeproc{ref-ElJundi2019hULMonA}{ElJundi et al., 2019};
\citeproc{ref-Michailidis2024Comparative}{Michailidis, 2024};
\citeproc{ref-Rafeef2023Evaluation}{Rafeef A. Hameed et al., 2023}),
including for lower-resource languages
(\citeproc{ref-Nazir2025Leveraging}{Nazir et al., 2025};
\citeproc{ref-Salahudeen2023HausaNLP}{Salahudeen et al., 2023};
\citeproc{ref-Tela2024Transferring}{Tela et al., 2024}) and via
cross-lingual transfer (\citeproc{ref-Kumar2021Sentiment}{Kumar \&
Albuquerque, 2021}; \citeproc{ref-Pan2023Evaluation}{Pan et al., 2023}).
This performance is costly: such models require large parameter counts
(\citeproc{ref-Francesco2021XLM}{Francesco et al., 2021};
\citeproc{ref-Rafeef2023Evaluation}{Rafeef A. Hameed et al., 2023}),
fine-tuning expertise (\citeproc{ref-Kotelnikova2023Cross}{Kotelnikova
et al., 2023}; \citeproc{ref-Priban2024comparative}{Přibáň et al.,
2024}), and substantial labeled training data
(\citeproc{ref-Mikhail2024Multilingual}{Mikhail et al., 2024};
\citeproc{ref-Salahudeen2023HausaNLP}{Salahudeen et al., 2023}) ---
barriers that put them out of reach for many social science researchers.

By contrast, general-purpose foundational LLMs used without fine-tuning
remain understudied for multilingual sentiment analysis. Preliminary
work with Mistral-7B, LLaMA2, and instruction-tuned models suggests
promise in Italian, Russian, and cross-lingual settings
(\citeproc{ref-Buscemi2024ChatGPT}{Buscemi \& Proverbio, 2024};
\citeproc{ref-Plagwitz2024Zero}{Plagwitz et al., 2024};
\citeproc{ref-Rusnachenko2024Large}{Rusnachenko et al., 2024};
\citeproc{ref-Venerito2024Large}{Venerito \& Iannone, 2024}). Evidence
on prompt language effects is mixed: some studies find language-specific
prompting advantages (\citeproc{ref-Hamalainen2022Video}{Hämäläinen et
al., 2022}; \citeproc{ref-Vileikyte2024Sentiment}{Vileikytė et al.,
2024}), while others report that English prompting broadly improves
cross-linguistic performance (\citeproc{ref-Jiang2020How}{Jiang et al.,
2020}; \citeproc{ref-Lin2022Few}{Lin et al., 2022};
\citeproc{ref-Muennighoff2023Crosslingual}{Muennighoff et al., 2023};
\citeproc{ref-Nie2024Decomposed}{Nie et al., 2024}). This study
addresses this gap by evaluating leading open- and closed-weight
foundational LLMs on French text.

\subsection{Translation}\label{translation}

To enable the EN→EN condition described below, the French corpus was
machine-translated into English using Google Translate's document
translation service, processed in batches via an automated R workflow
that preserved document IDs to keep French and English versions aligned
in the final dataset.\footnote{See
  \texttt{src/21\_translate\_to\_english.R} in the project repository
  for the full translation pipeline.}

\subsection{Manual Annotation}\label{manual-annotation}

A validation benchmark of 200 sentences was randomly sampled from the
corpus, and each sentence was machine-translated into English via Google
Translate and the \texttt{polyglotR} package. This sample size falls
within the 100 to 300 units generally recommended for validating
automated sentiment measures against a manually coded gold standard
(\citeproc{ref-vanAtteveldt2021validity}{van Atteveldt et al., 2021}),
and yields 95\% confidence intervals of roughly ±0.05 around a
correlation of 0.80. Three independent coders rated each sentence's
sentiment on a scale from -1 (very negative) to 1 (very positive) using
a custom Shiny application with keyboard shortcuts and scale reminders,
and the three ratings were averaged to obtain the ground truth used to
evaluate both dictionary-based and LLM methods.

Agreement between individual coders was moderate. Krippendorff's alpha
(interval) was 0.597 (95\% bootstrap CI {[}0.516, 0.656{]}), the mean
pairwise correlation between coders was 0.687, and all three coders
assigned the same sign (negative, zero, or positive) to only 42.5\% of
sentences. Two features of the task account for much of this: coders
used a fine-grained scale (more than 60 distinct values), and most
sentences carry mild sentiment, so small differences often cross zero.
Because the ground truth averages three coders, it is considerably more
reliable than any single coder: the Spearman-Brown projection from the
mean pairwise correlation puts the reliability of the three-coder mean
at 0.868.

\subsection{Dictionary-Based Sentiment
Analysis}\label{dictionary-based-sentiment-analysis}

Lexicon-based sentiment scoring was applied to both the French original
and the English translation as a classical baseline. For French text, a
customized version of the French Lexicoder Sentiment Dictionary
(\texttt{frlsd}) (\citeproc{ref-duval_petry16}{Duval \& Pétry, 2016})
was applied to lowercased, stopword-removed, sentence-segmented text,
scoring each sentence as (positive terms − negative terms) / total
words. For English translations, the standard Lexicoder Sentiment
Dictionary (\citeproc{ref-young_andsoroka12}{Young \& Soroka, 2012}) was
used with additional handling for contractions, proper nouns, and
negation (e.g., ``not good'' scored as negative), scoring each sentence
as ((positive + negated\_negative) − (negative + negated\_positive)) /
total words.

\subsection{LLM-Based Sentiment
Analysis}\label{llm-based-sentiment-analysis}

Twelve general-purpose foundational LLMs were evaluated, selected for
prominence and affordability --- realistic choices for researchers who
need to analyze large corpora rather than a single sample. Specialized
fine-tuned models can outperform them on narrow tasks, but at the cost
in expertise and infrastructure set out above.

The evaluation spans nine open-weight models --- Llama 3.2 1B, Llama 3.2
3B, Llama 3.1 8B, GPT-OSS 20B, Qwen3 32B, Llama 4 Scout, GPT-OSS 120B,
Qwen3 235B-A22B, and DeepSeek V3.2, ranging from 1B to 671B total
parameters --- and three closed-weight models: Claude Haiku 4.5
(Anthropic), Gemini 3.5 Flash (Google), and GPT-5.6 Luna (OpenAI). This
lineup updates an initial 2024-2025 evaluation of 11 comparable models
using an identical experimental design; several models from that
original evaluation (e.g., GPT-4o, Gemini 2.0 Flash) had already been
deprecated by their providers within eighteen months, underscoring how
quickly this landscape moves. The smallest model, Llama 3.2 1B, rarely
followed the instruction to answer with a number: about one call in 23
returned a parseable score, and even after retries it produced a usable
score for only 58 to 74 of the 200 sentences, depending on the
condition. It was retained deliberately: while models of around 8B
parameters can handle the task, a 1B-parameter model appears too small
to follow the instructions reliably at all, which marks a practical
lower bound on model size. Its metrics rest on a reduced and non-random
subset of sentences and are not directly comparable to those of the
other models.

Each model was tested under three conditions to isolate the effects of
prompt language and text language: French prompts on French text
(FR→FR), English prompts on French text (EN→FR), and English prompts on
the machine-translated English text (EN→EN).

All prompts followed a standardized structure: a task definition, a
five-point sentiment scale from -1.0 to 1.0 with anchor descriptions
(allowing intermediate values), an instruction to consider cultural and
linguistic nuance, and a constraint to respond with only a numerical
value. French prompts were direct translations of the English prompts.

Each model was queried via the \texttt{ellmer} R package, using the
vendors' own APIs for the three closed-weight models and OpenRouter for
the nine open-weight models, with responses capped at 400 tokens.
Because OpenRouter can serve the same model through several providers at
different numerical precisions, each open-weight model was pinned to a
single named provider endpoint, at the highest precision offered, with
fallback to other providers disabled.\footnote{Endpoint pins, and the
  reasoning settings used to suppress chain-of-thought in models that
  reason by default, are listed in \texttt{src/94\_models\_map.R}.}
Gemini 3.5 Flash was queried with its default settings, under which it
generates reasoning tokens before answering (354 output tokens per call
on average, against 7 for Claude Haiku 4.5). Each sentence was scored
three times per model-condition combination (21,600 scores in total),
and the mean of the three runs was taken as the final score. Despite
explicit instructions to respond with a bare number, models frequently
returned formatted text (e.g., ``Sentiment: 0.5''); these responses were
parsed with a pattern-matching function validated against the expected
-1.0 to 1.0 range.

\subsection{Validation and Performance
Evaluation}\label{validation-and-performance-evaluation}

Performance was assessed with five complementary metrics: Pearson
correlation between each model's continuous predictions and the manually
annotated ground truth; Lin's concordance correlation coefficient (CCC),
which, unlike Pearson correlation, also penalizes differences in scale
and location, and so captures whether predictions reproduce the
intensity of human ratings rather than only their ordering; mean
absolute error (MAE); weighted F1 score on a 7-category classification
(``very\_negative'' through ``very\_positive''); and weighted F1 score
on a simplified 3-category classification (negative/neutral/positive)
that groups the 7 categories into broad polarity bands. Weighted (rather
than macro) F1 is used because the manually coded categories are
unevenly distributed --- ``somewhat\_positive'' alone accounts for 44\%
of annotated sentences, while ``very\_negative'' and ``very\_positive''
each account for 1\% or less --- so a weighted average better reflects
overall classification quality given this class imbalance. Comparing the
two classification schemes separates fine-grained sentiment
discrimination (7-category) from broad polarity classification
(3-category). All metrics were calculated on complete cases only.

Condition comparisons are paired by model. Alongside paired t-tests, two
one-sided tests (TOST) against an equivalence bound of ±0.05 on each
metric's scale assess whether a difference is small enough to be treated
as negligible, since a non-significant t-test cannot on its own
establish the absence of an effect. These comparisons and the averages
reported in the Results cover the eleven models that scored all 200
sentences (33 model-condition combinations) and exclude the dictionary
baselines, which have no prompt. Llama 3.2 1B is reported separately: it
scored a different, partial subset of sentences in each condition, so
its differences across conditions would compare unlike samples.

\section{Results}\label{results}

\subsection{Prompt Language Has No Meaningful Effect;
Machine-Translating the Text
Does}\label{prompt-language-has-no-meaningful-effect-machine-translating-the-text-does}

Holding the text constant, prompt language made no detectable
difference. French prompts on French text (FR→FR) edged out English
prompts on the same text (EN→FR) on every metric --- mean correlation
0.700 vs.~0.673, MAE 0.209 vs.~0.217, 3-category F1 0.669 vs.~0.646,
7-category F1 0.364 vs.~0.347 --- but none of these paired differences
was statistically significant (all \emph{p} \textgreater{} 0.12).
Equivalence tests go further: FR→FR and EN→FR were statistically
equivalent within ±0.05 on MAE (\emph{p} = 0.002), 7-category F1
(\emph{p} = 0.034), and 3-category F1 (\emph{p} = 0.034). Correlation
was the one metric where equivalence could not be established: the 90\%
confidence interval of the difference, {[}−0.032, 0.085{]}, leaves room
for a French-prompt advantage of up to 0.085, but not for an
English-prompt advantage larger than 0.032. H1 is therefore not
supported.

Machine-translating the text into English, by contrast, had a measurable
cost. Relative to FR→FR, scoring the English translation (EN→EN) lowered
mean correlation by 0.053 (0.700 vs.~0.647, \emph{p} = 0.007) and
3-category F1 by 0.063 (0.669 vs.~0.606, \emph{p} \textless{} 0.001);
the 3-category loss also held against EN→FR (0.041, \emph{p} = 0.005).
Differences in MAE and 7-category F1 across all three conditions
remained within the ±0.05 equivalence bound. The translation penalty is
therefore concentrated in how well models rank sentences by sentiment
and identify their polarity, consistent with sentiment altered in
machine translation (\citeproc{ref-Mohammad2016translation}{Mohammad et
al., 2016}).

\subsection{LLMs Are a Viable Alternative to Dictionary
Methods}\label{llms-are-a-viable-alternative-to-dictionary-methods}

General-purpose LLMs, used without fine-tuning, correlated with human
judgments well above the dictionary baselines. The best configurations
exceeded r = 0.80: GPT-5.6 Luna led on correlation (r = 0.810 in FR→FR)
and concordance (CCC = 0.779), Claude Haiku 4.5 on both 3-category F1
(0.778) and 7-category F1 (0.537, both FR→FR), and Gemini 3.5 Flash on
MAE (0.156 in EN→FR). Nine of the 33 model-condition combinations
correlated with the ground truth more strongly than individual human
coders agreed with the mean of the other two (mean leave-one-out r =
0.748, range 0.724--0.779).\footnote{This comparison favors the models
  slightly, since the ground truth they are compared against averages
  three coders rather than two.}

Closed-weight models outperformed open-weight ones on average (mean r =
0.768 vs.~0.638; 3-category F1 0.689 vs.~0.622), but the gap closed at
the top of the open-weight range: Qwen3 235B-A22B reached r = 0.774 in
FR→FR, above Gemini 3.5 Flash in the same condition, and GPT-OSS 120B
and GPT-OSS 20B averaged r = 0.733 and 0.716 across conditions.
Performance degraded steadily below that. Llama 3.1 8B remained usable
(r = 0.575--0.612), Llama 3.2 3B was erratic (r = 0.476 in FR→FR but
0.132 in EN→FR), and Llama 3.2 1B failed outright: it returned a usable
score for only 58 to 74 sentences per condition, and on those its
correlation with the ground truth ranged from −0.199 to 0.100.

The English Lexicoder Sentiment Dictionary reached a correlation of
0.548 and the French version 0.357, against a mean of 0.673 for the
eleven LLMs; on 3-category F1 the dictionaries (0.588 and 0.557) also
trailed the LLM mean (0.640), though the English dictionary outperformed
Llama 3.2 3B and Qwen3 32B. The gap is widest on concordance: the LLMs
averaged a CCC of 0.631, against 0.233 and 0.181 for the English and
French dictionaries. On MAE, however, both dictionaries (0.203 and
0.210) did slightly better than the LLM average (0.213). This reflects
the dictionaries' compressed output rather than accuracy: their scores
cluster near zero (SD = 0.065 for the English dictionary, vs.~0.286 for
the ground truth and 0.38--0.47 for the LLMs), which keeps absolute
errors small on a sample dominated by mildly positive sentences while
discriminating poorly between them.

\subsection{Classification
Granularity}\label{classification-granularity}

All models performed substantially better on 3-category than 7-category
classification (best F1 of 0.778 vs.~0.537; mean 0.640 vs.~0.349; see
Figure 3, below). Models most reliably identified positive and negative
sentiment (7-category F1 = 0.482 and 0.441, rising to 0.690 and 0.627 in
the 3-category scheme) and struggled most with the extreme categories
(very\_negative F1 = 0.256, very\_positive F1 = 0.124), which are nearly
absent from the sample (two sentences and one sentence respectively).
Neutral remained the hardest 3-category label (F1 = 0.258). Researchers
needing fine-grained sentiment distinctions may find general-purpose
LLMs insufficient without further adaptation; the broader
positive/neutral/negative distinction is handled far more reliably.

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

The central finding is that prompt language does not meaningfully affect
LLM sentiment analysis performance on French text --- a more nuanced
picture than the English-prompting advantage reported elsewhere
(\citeproc{ref-Jiang2020How}{Jiang et al., 2020};
\citeproc{ref-Lin2022Few}{Lin et al., 2022};
\citeproc{ref-Muennighoff2023Crosslingual}{Muennighoff et al., 2023}).
If anything, the point estimates favored French prompts, and equivalence
tests ruled out any English-prompt advantage larger than a few
hundredths on every metric. What did cost performance was
machine-translating the text itself: scoring English translations
lowered correlation and 3-category F1 relative to working with the
French original. This challenges the assumption that researchers must
work in English to get good results on French text: francophone
researchers can prompt in French with no analytical penalty, and teams
can choose between French and English prompts by convenience rather than
performance. The practical warning runs the other way:
machine-translating a French corpus into English before analysis is not
neutral. Theoretically, the case of French suggests that the ``English
prompt advantage'' documented in prior work may be task-dependent,
language-pair specific, or diminishing as models improve; whether it
holds for other languages remains open.

This finding is only practically useful if LLMs are themselves viable
for the task, and the results support that they are. Leading
closed-weight models achieved strong correlations (GPT-5.6 Luna, r =
0.810) and 3-category F1 scores (Claude Haiku 4.5, 0.778) without any
task-specific fine-tuning, several configurations agreed with the ground
truth more closely than individual human coders did, and the largest
open-weight models came close to them (Qwen3 235B-A22B, r = 0.774).
Model size sets a floor, however. Models from roughly 8B parameters
upward produced usable scores, a 3B model was unreliable across
conditions, and a 1B model could not follow the instruction to return a
number at all. Cost does not stand in the way: at list prices, scoring
1,000 sentences cost between USD 0.02 and 0.40 for every model but one
(Table~\ref{tbl-cost}). The exception, Gemini 3.5 Flash (USD 3.77),
reflects the reasoning tokens it generates by default rather than its
per-token price. Together, these results mean social scientists can
confidently deploy leading closed-weight LLMs, and larger open-weight
ones, for sentiment analysis of French text with French or English
prompts, whichever suits their workflow, without fine-tuning expertise
or heavy computational resources
(\citeproc{ref-Priban2024comparative}{Přibáň et al., 2024}) --- provided
they avoid the smallest models.

Two secondary results qualify this picture. First, LLMs clearly
outperformed the Lexicoder Sentiment Dictionary on correlation (mean LLM
r = 0.673 vs.~0.548 for the English dictionary) and, more modestly, on
3-category F1 (0.640 vs.~0.588), but the dictionaries' near-zero output
gave them a slightly lower MAE --- a reminder that MAE alone can reward
a method that barely discriminates. Second, all models performed
markedly worse on fine-grained 7-category classification than on
3-category polarity (mean F1 of 0.349 vs.~0.640), struggling most with
extreme sentiment categories.

These two results are compatible once ordering and calibration are
distinguished. LLMs capture sentiment intensity in the sense of ordering
sentences from least to most positive and reproducing the spread of
human ratings: their concordance with the ground truth (mean CCC =
0.631, up to 0.779) far exceeds that of the dictionaries (0.233 and
0.181), whose scores barely depart from zero. What LLMs do poorly is
place scores on the right side of fixed category thresholds. They
overstate extremes --- 10.8\% of LLM scores exceed ±0.66, against 1.5\%
of ground-truth values --- so a sentence rated 0.5 by humans and 0.8 by
a model is correctly ordered but misclassified. The ground truth
contributes to the mismatch: as an average of three coders, it is pulled
toward the center, leaving only three sentences in the two extreme
categories. A transparent, fully interpretable dictionary therefore
remains a defensible baseline for basic polarity detection but not for
measuring intensity, and researchers needing fixed fine-grained
categories should not expect general-purpose LLMs to deliver them out of
the box, though Claude Haiku 4.5's stronger 7-category performance
(0.537) suggests that calibration varies across models.

\section{Limitations}\label{limitations}

Several limitations qualify these findings. The 200-sentence benchmark
is adequate for comparing models but thin at the extremes of the scale:
only two sentences fall in the very negative category and one in the
very positive, so class-level F1 scores for these categories are
unstable. The benchmark is also demanding for humans: agreement between
individual coders was moderate (Krippendorff's alpha = 0.597), and
averaging three coders, while it yields a more reliable ground truth,
also pulls ratings toward the center and makes the extreme categories
scarcer still. Finally, the findings concern French only. The corpus is
French-language news on a single technical/political domain (open-source
software), so neither the performance hierarchy nor the absence of a
prompt-language effect can be assumed to extend to other languages,
especially those less represented in LLM training data, or to other
genres such as social media or political debate.

Methodologically, the EN→EN condition relies on Google Translate, and
translation quality was deliberately not validated: the condition
reproduces what a researcher who does not read English can do, namely
translate automatically and analyze the result. Its lower performance
should accordingly be read as the cost of that workflow rather than of
translation as such. Model selection prioritized affordability and
prominence over including every top-performing model globally,
reflecting the perspective of researchers who need to analyze corpora of
millions of sentences rather than a few hundred. A single standardized
prompt was used across all models for comparability, which may
understate peak performance achievable through model-specific prompt
engineering; the need for a response-parsing function also indicates
models did not always follow the requested output format exactly.

Costs are reported at list prices and count cached prompt tokens at the
full input rate, so they are upper bounds; actual invoices depend on
provider-specific caching discounts. More fundamentally, this evaluation
is a snapshot: given how quickly LLM capabilities and provider offerings
change --- several models evaluated in an earlier version of this study
were deprecated within eighteen months --- the specific rankings
reported here should be expected to age quickly, even as the
higher-level finding regarding prompt language in French is likely to
hold across model generations.

\section{Conclusion}\label{conclusion}

For French text, prompt language does not constrain how social
scientists work with LLMs. Francophone teams are free to prompt in
French, and the choice between French and English prompts ceases to be a
design decision that needs defending. Machine translation is another
matter: translating the corpus into English before analysis, a step that
has often preceded computational work on non-English text, lowered
performance here and loses its rationale.

That opens work this study could not do. Whether the absence of a
prompt-language effect holds beyond French --- particularly for
languages less represented in training data, where the English-prompting
advantage was first reported --- is the natural next test, and this
design ports to it directly: the same three conditions, the same
manually annotated benchmark, a different target language. If it does,
comparative projects spanning several languages could let each team
prompt in its own language and pool the results.

Method choice remains a judgment about research goals rather than a
ranking. Dictionary methods retain an advantage these results do not
overturn --- every score they produce can be traced to the words that
generated it --- and that transparency is worth more than accuracy in
some research designs. What the evidence here settles is narrower and,
for researchers working with French text, more useful: the language of
the prompt is not among the things that need to be traded off.
