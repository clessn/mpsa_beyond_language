\abstract{For a decade, comparative text analysis had one answer to
non-English text, which was to translate it into English and apply
English tools. Large language models (LLMs) inherit that reflex, and a
second one, to prompt in English. This article tests both on French
news. Fifteen open- and closed-weight models scored 200 hand-coded
sentences under three workflows, a French prompt on the original, an
English prompt on the original, and an English prompt on a machine
translation. Prompt language made no difference. Equivalence tests rule
out any substantial advantage for English, and the equivalence holds on
the full corpus of 2,683 articles. Translation did make a difference.
The same machine translation that improved the Lexicoder dictionary
lowered the LLMs' agreement with human coders, so the strategy that
served English-bound tools becomes a cost for models that read the
source language. Every model from about 8B parameters upward beat both
the native French dictionary and the translated English one on
correlation with human coders, reaching r = 0.81 against 0.36, for a few
cents per thousand sentences and without any training data or
fine-tuning. The language of the prompt is a matter of convenience. The
language of the text is not: analyze it in the original. }

\keywords{Sentiment Analysis, Large Language Models, Machine
Translation, Prompt Language, French Language, Open-Weight
Models, Computational Social Science}

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

For ten years, the advice to a researcher facing a corpus in French,
German, or Arabic was simple: translate it into English and use the
English tools. Computational text analysis grew up around English, and
its tools, from sentiment dictionaries to topic models, were built for
English text. This dominance has been criticized for depressing
comparative research and for excluding scholarly communities that work
in other languages (\citeproc{ref-badenThreeGapsComputational2022}{Baden
et al., 2022}). Machine translation was the workaround, and it was
validated repeatedly for dictionaries and topic models
(\citeproc{ref-devriesNoLongerLost2018}{De Vries et al., 2018};
\citeproc{ref-lucasComputerAssistedTextAnalysis2015}{Lucas et al.,
2015}). Large language models (LLMs) change the terms of the problem.
Used without task-specific training, they match or exceed crowd workers
and expert coders on common annotation tasks
(\citeproc{ref-gilardiChatGPTOutperformsCrowd2023}{Gilardi et al.,
2023}; \citeproc{ref-tornbergLargeLanguageModels2025}{T"ornberg, 2025}),
and they do so, with some loss, in languages other than English
(\citeproc{ref-heseltineLargeLanguageModels2024}{Heseltine \& Clemm Von
Hohenberg, 2024}; \citeproc{ref-rathjeGPTEffectiveTool2024}{Rathje et
al., 2024}). For the many researchers who work in a language other than
English and have neither the labeled data nor the skills to train or
fine-tune a model, this is the first tool that reads their texts as they
are. This article shows that the old advice now points the wrong way.

Yet researchers who use LLMs on non-English text often keep two reflexes
from the English-centered era. The first is to prompt in English. The
justification is that these models are trained mostly on English
(\citeproc{ref-touvron_etal23a}{Touvron et al., 2023}) and that English
instructions have been found to work better even for non-English inputs
(\citeproc{ref-laiChatGPTEnglishComprehensive2023}{Lai et al., 2023};
\citeproc{ref-Lin2022Few}{X. V. Lin et al., 2022}). English prompts are
also cheaper, because tokenizers trained on English-heavy data split
French into more pieces (\citeproc{ref-ahiaAllLanguagesCost2023}{Ahia et
al., 2023}). The second is to translate the text into English before
analysis. This practice comes from dictionaries and other bag-of-words
methods, whose lexicons were English, and it reappears as
``translate-test'' with LLMs
(\citeproc{ref-Etxaniz2024Multilingual}{Etxaniz et al., 2024}). Whether
either reflex helps, harms, or makes no difference is unsettled, and the
social science evidence is thin.

This article tests both reflexes on sentiment analysis, a measurement
task central to political communication research
(\citeproc{ref-vanAtteveldt2021validity}{van Atteveldt et al., 2021};
\citeproc{ref-young_andsoroka12}{Young \& Soroka, 2012}). Sentiment is a
demanding test for translation, because affective meaning is precisely
what translation can alter
(\citeproc{ref-Mohammad2016translation}{Mohammad et al., 2016}). The
benchmark is a set of 200 sentences drawn from 2,683 articles on
open-source software published by 13 French-language dailies in Québec
and France, each sentence scored by three human coders. Fifteen open-
and closed-weight language models scored them with a French prompt, with
an English prompt, and with an English prompt on a machine translation.
The French and English Lexicoder dictionaries provide the comparison
that most applied researchers face. Equivalence tests establish the
absence of a prompt-language effect as a finding rather than a
non-result, and the best model then rescored the full corpus of 2,683
articles to check that the result holds at scale.

The results speak to both reflexes. Prompt language made no meaningful
difference, though the English prompt is cheaper. Translation did: the
same machine translation that lifted the dictionary workflow from r =
0.36 to 0.55 lowered every model of at least 8B parameters when the
English prompt was held constant, and the models beat both dictionaries
without it. The article also answers the practical question that
follows: is a researcher working on French text still better served by a
dictionary? On this evidence, no. Every model from about 8B parameters
upward outperformed both the native French dictionary and the translated
English one on correlation with human judgments, open-weight models
included, for a few cents per thousand sentences.

\section{Literature Review}\label{literature-review}

\subsection{LLMs as Measurement Instruments in the Social
Sciences}\label{llms-as-measurement-instruments-in-the-social-sciences}

Zero-shot LLM annotation has moved quickly from curiosity to candidate
standard. The paper that set the agenda had ChatGPT annotate 6,183
English tweets and news articles without any example. Its accuracy
exceeded that of crowd workers by about 25 percentage points on average,
its intercoder agreement exceeded that of both crowd workers and trained
annotators on every task, and each annotation cost about thirty times
less than paying a crowd worker
(\citeproc{ref-gilardiChatGPTOutperformsCrowd2023}{Gilardi et al.,
2023}). GPT-4 outperformed expert coders and supervised classifiers at
identifying the party of politicians from their social media posts in
eleven countries
(\citeproc{ref-tornbergLargeLanguageModels2025}{T"ornberg, 2025}). GPT-4
also closely approximated expert coding of negativity and sentiment in
tweets from the United States, Chile, Germany, and Italy
(\citeproc{ref-heseltineLargeLanguageModels2024}{Heseltine \& Clemm Von
Hohenberg, 2024}). These results have fed broad claims that generative
AI could transform computational social science
(\citeproc{ref-ziemsCanLargeLanguage2024}{Ziems et al., 2024}), chiefly
by removing the need for the thousands of annotated examples that
supervised learning requires
(\citeproc{ref-laurerLessAnnotatingMore2024}{Laurer et al., 2024}).

A more cautious literature qualifies these claims on three fronts.
First, performance is uneven. Across 25 English benchmarks, zero-shot
LLMs did not outperform the best fine-tuned models on classification
tasks (\citeproc{ref-ziemsCanLargeLanguage2024}{Ziems et al., 2024}).
When GPT-4 replicated 27 annotation tasks from 11 datasets in published
social science articles, its performance varied with the dataset and
with the kind of task, and on a third of the tasks the model missed or
mislabeled at least half of the relevant cases
(\citeproc{ref-pangakisAutomatedAnnotationGenerative2023}{Pangakis et
al., 2023}). Validation against human coding therefore remains
indispensable (\citeproc{ref-grimmer_stewart13}{Grimmer \& Stewart,
2013}; \citeproc{ref-linNavigatingRisksUsing2026}{H. Lin \& Zhang,
2026}), and it is only as informative as the human gold standard is
reliable (\citeproc{ref-songValidationsWeTrust2020}{Song et al., 2020}).
Second, established tools retain advantages. Dictionaries generalize
across domains and preserve gradations of sentiment
(\citeproc{ref-vanderveenAdvantagesLexiconbasedSentiment2025}{Van Der
Veen \& Bleich, 2025}). However, off-the-shelf dictionaries validate
poorly against human judgment
(\citeproc{ref-vanAtteveldt2021validity}{van Atteveldt et al., 2021}),
and transformer-based models outperform them in German political and
cultural texts (\citeproc{ref-klahnDictionariesLLMsEvaluation2025}{Klähn
et al., 2025};
\citeproc{ref-widmannCreatingComparingDictionary2023}{Widmann \& Wich,
2023}). A domain-specific multilingual classifier can also match GPT-4o
at a fraction of the cost
(\citeproc{ref-mochtakParlasentMappingSentiment2025}{Mochtak et al.,
2025}). Third, reliance on proprietary models threatens reproducibility
(\citeproc{ref-spirlingWhyOpensourceGenerative2023}{Spirling, 2023}).
This has prompted calls to justify their use
(\citeproc{ref-palmerUsingProprietaryLanguage2023}{Palmer et al., 2023})
and evidence that open-weight models can be competitive alternatives
(\citeproc{ref-klahnDictionariesLLMsEvaluation2025}{Klähn et al.,
2025}).

These debates leave the question of language largely implicit. The
broadest evaluations rely on English benchmarks
(\citeproc{ref-ziemsCanLargeLanguage2024}{Ziems et al., 2024}). Studies
that extend to other languages report slightly lower performance on
non-English text
(\citeproc{ref-heseltineLargeLanguageModels2024}{Heseltine \& Clemm Von
Hohenberg, 2024}), or lower accuracy outside the United States that the
authors attribute to party systems rather than to language
(\citeproc{ref-tornbergLargeLanguageModels2025}{T"ornberg, 2025}).
Neither examines how researchers should interact with models in those
languages. The most extensive multilingual evaluation in the social
sciences covers 12 languages, yet it compared GPT with dictionary
methods on English headlines only
(\citeproc{ref-rathjeGPTEffectiveTool2024}{Rathje et al., 2024}).
Researchers working in other languages must make two choices: the
language of the prompt and the language of the text.

\subsection{The Language of the
Prompt}\label{the-language-of-the-prompt}

The case for prompting in English starts from two observations. LLMs are
trained predominantly on English (\citeproc{ref-touvron_etal23a}{Touvron
et al., 2023}), and they perform less well in other languages,
especially lower-resourced ones
(\citeproc{ref-ahujaMEGAMultilingualEvaluation2023}{Ahuja et al., 2023};
\citeproc{ref-laiChatGPTEnglishComprehensive2023}{Lai et al., 2023}). A
mechanistic account suggests why. Llama-2 models seem to think in
English before answering in another language. Given a French prompt, the
model works out the meaning in its middle layers, and at that stage its
internal state already points to the English word for the answer. Only
in the last layers does it switch to the target language
(\citeproc{ref-wendler_etal24}{Wendler et al., 2024}). If models
effectively reason through English, instructions written in English
should be understood best. Several findings support this pivot-language
view. ChatGPT performed better with English prompts even when the task
and input were in other languages
(\citeproc{ref-laiChatGPTEnglishComprehensive2023}{Lai et al., 2023}).
English templates remedied the unexpectedly low accuracy of prompts
handcrafted by native speakers (\citeproc{ref-Lin2022Few}{X. V. Lin et
al., 2022}). Models fine-tuned on English prompts performed best with
English prompts even on non-English samples
(\citeproc{ref-Muennighoff2023Crosslingual}{Muennighoff et al., 2023}).
English instructions outperformed Arabic ones on average across eleven
Arabic classification tasks (\citeproc{ref-Kmainasi2025Native}{Kmainasi
et al., 2025}).

A competing view emphasizes multilingual competence. Large models show
strong multilingual reasoning abilities even in underrepresented
languages (\citeproc{ref-shiLanguageModelsAre2022}{Shi et al., 2022}).
For capable models, the language of the prompt appears to matter little.
In the Arabic study just cited, differences across prompt languages were
minimal for the strongest model, GPT-4o
(\citeproc{ref-Kmainasi2025Native}{Kmainasi et al., 2025}). GPT-4
Turbo's labels for Indonesian tweets under English and Indonesian
prompts agreed almost perfectly
(\citeproc{ref-rathjeGPTEffectiveTool2024}{Rathje et al., 2024}). The
evidence on both sides comes mostly from reasoning and classification
benchmarks, older models, and lower-resourced languages. None of it
tests whether prompt language affects the accuracy of a graded sentiment
judgment in a well-resourced language. The two views imply competing
expectations:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{H1a (pivot language).} English prompts yield more accurate
sentiment measurement of French text than French prompts.

\textbf{H1b (multilingual competence).} English and French prompts yield
equivalent performance on French text.
\end{quote}

\subsection{The Language of the Text}\label{the-language-of-the-text}

The second reflex predates LLMs. The bridge strategy of translating
multilingual corpora into English so that English-language tools can be
applied (\citeproc{ref-lucasComputerAssistedTextAnalysis2015}{Lucas et
al., 2015}) has been validated repeatedly. Machine-translated and
human-translated texts yield highly similar term-document matrices and
topic models (\citeproc{ref-devriesNoLongerLost2018}{De Vries et al.,
2018}). A machine translation of the Lexicoder dictionary captured
legislative conflict across European parliaments
(\citeproc{ref-prokschMultilingualSentimentAnalysis2019}{Proksch et al.,
2019}). For sentiment specifically, analysis of English translations of
Arabic posts was competitive with analysis of the originals
(\citeproc{ref-Mohammad2016translation}{Mohammad et al., 2016}). The
logic has carried into the LLM era. Translating inputs into English
before inference improves the performance of multilingual models
(\citeproc{ref-Etxaniz2024Multilingual}{Etxaniz et al., 2024}). It is
often hard to beat for lower-resourced languages written in non-Latin
scripts (\citeproc{ref-ahujaMEGAMultilingualEvaluation2023}{Ahuja et
al., 2023}).

The opposing view treats translation as a source of loss. Translation
alters sentiment: affective words disappear or are replaced by words of
opposite valence, ambiguous words are mistranslated, and culturally
specific expressions lose their meaning
(\citeproc{ref-Mohammad2016translation}{Mohammad et al., 2016}).
Approaches that avoid translation, such as multilingual sentence
embeddings, yield comparable or more reliable results in some settings
(\citeproc{ref-lichtCrossLingualClassificationPolitical2023}{Licht,
2023}). Crucially, the case for translation was built largely on tools
that cannot read the source language, such as English dictionaries and
bag-of-words models. LLMs can. For them, the supporting evidence comes
from benchmarks whose non-English versions were themselves created by
translation, a setting that may favor translation-based methods, as the
authors note (\citeproc{ref-Etxaniz2024Multilingual}{Etxaniz et al.,
2024}). It does not come from text written natively in the source
language. Again, the two views imply competing expectations:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{H2a (translation as bridge).} Analyzing English machine
translations yields sentiment measurement at least as accurate as
analyzing the French original.

\textbf{H2b (translation as loss).} Analyzing English machine
translations yields less accurate sentiment measurement than analyzing
the French original.
\end{quote}

\subsection{Research Questions}\label{research-questions}

These debates motivate two research questions. \textbf{RQ1:} Does the
language used to interact with the model affect LLM sentiment
measurement of French text? This covers the language of the prompt and
the language of the text through machine translation. H1a--b and H2a--b
offer competing answers. \textbf{RQ2:} Can general-purpose LLMs, open-
and closed-weight,\footnote{``Open source'' traditionally implies access
  aligned with free and open-source software principles. In the LLM
  context, the term usually refers to the public release of model
  weights. ``Open-weight'' describes this condition more accurately and
  is used throughout.} measure sentiment in French text well enough,
without fine-tuning, to be a viable alternative to dictionary-based
methods?

\subsection{Translation}\label{translation}

The EN→EN condition described below reproduces the
translate-then-analyze strategy reviewed above. For this condition, the
French corpus was machine-translated into English with Google
Translate's document translation service. An automated R workflow
processed the documents in batches and preserved document IDs, so that
the French and English versions remain aligned.\footnote{See
  \texttt{src/21\_translate\_to\_english.R} in the project repository.}

\subsection{Manual Annotation}\label{manual-annotation}

A validation benchmark of 200 sentences was randomly sampled from the
corpus. The sentences come from 194 different articles (six articles
contribute two) and average 23 words (median 21, range 1 to 73). Each
sentence was machine-translated into English with Google Translate
through the \texttt{polyglotR} package. This sample size falls within
the 100 to 300 units generally recommended for validating automated
sentiment measures against a manually coded gold standard
(\citeproc{ref-vanAtteveldt2021validity}{van Atteveldt et al., 2021}).
It yields 95\% confidence intervals of roughly ±0.05 around a
correlation of 0.80. Three members of the research team rated the
sentiment of each sentence independently, on a scale from -1 (very
negative) to 1 (very positive), in a custom Shiny application that
displayed the sentence and its English translation and no model output.
The three ratings were averaged. This average is the ground truth
against which both dictionary and LLM methods are evaluated.

Agreement between individual coders was moderate. Krippendorff's alpha
(interval) was 0.597 (95\% bootstrap CI {[}0.516, 0.656{]}). The mean
pairwise correlation between coders was 0.687. All three coders assigned
the same sign (negative, zero, or positive) to only 42.5\% of sentences.
Two features of the task account for much of this disagreement. Coders
used a fine-grained scale with more than 60 distinct values, and most
sentences carry mild sentiment, so small differences often cross zero.
The ground truth averages three coders and is therefore more reliable
than any single coder: the Spearman-Brown projection from the mean
pairwise correlation puts the reliability of the three-coder mean at
0.868.

\subsection{Dictionary-Based Sentiment
Analysis}\label{dictionary-based-sentiment-analysis}

Lexicon-based scoring was applied to the French original and to the
English translation as a classical baseline. For French text, the French
Lexicoder Sentiment Dictionary (\citeproc{ref-duval_petry16}{Duval \&
Pétry, 2016}) was loaded as a quanteda dictionary without any change to
its word lists and applied to lowercased, stopword-removed,
sentence-segmented text, without negation handling. Each sentence was
scored as (positive terms − negative terms) / total words. For the
English translation, the standard Lexicoder Sentiment Dictionary
(\citeproc{ref-young_andsoroka12}{Young \& Soroka, 2012}) was used, with
additional handling of contractions, proper nouns, and negation (for
example, ``not good'' scored as negative). Each sentence was scored as
((positive + negated negative) − (negative + negated positive)) / total
words. The two dictionaries therefore differ in construction as well as
in language, and the negation handling, available only for English, can
only favor the English dictionary.

\subsection{LLM-Based Sentiment
Analysis}\label{llm-based-sentiment-analysis}

Fifteen general-purpose language models were evaluated. They were
selected for prominence and affordability, as realistic choices for
researchers who need to analyze large corpora. Three closed-weight
models from the major vendors are included as a reference point, the
justification that Palmer et al.
(\citeproc{ref-palmerUsingProprietaryLanguage2023}{2023}) ask for: the
studies that established LLM annotation in the social sciences relied on
proprietary GPT models
(\citeproc{ref-gilardiChatGPTOutperformsCrowd2023}{Gilardi et al.,
2023}; \citeproc{ref-heseltineLargeLanguageModels2024}{Heseltine \&
Clemm Von Hohenberg, 2024};
\citeproc{ref-rathjeGPTEffectiveTool2024}{Rathje et al., 2024};
\citeproc{ref-tornbergLargeLanguageModels2025}{T"ornberg, 2025}). Three
further models test specific claims. Mistral AI, a French developer,
describes its models as particularly strong in French. It also reports
that its Tekken tokenizer compresses French text about 30\% more
efficiently than its earlier tokenizer
(\citeproc{ref-mistralaiMistralNeMo2024}{Mistral AI, 2024}). Two Mistral
models test both claims.

The ten open-weight models are Llama 3.2 1B, Llama 3.2 3B, Llama 3.1 8B,
GPT-OSS 20B, Mistral Small 3.2 24B, Qwen3 32B, Llama 4 Scout, GPT-OSS
120B, Qwen3 235B-A22B, and DeepSeek V3.2. They range from 1B to 671B
total parameters. The five closed-weight models are Claude Haiku 4.5
(Anthropic), Gemini 3.5 Flash (Google), GPT-5.6 Luna (OpenAI), Mistral
Large 4 (Mistral AI), and Jev 1.13 (TypeSafe). Mistral Large 4 was
evaluated in its October 2026 preview release and is classified as
closed-weight because its weights were not yet published. Jev is
included because scoring by probabilities rather than by generated text
is a different way of measuring a continuous construct. It is not a text
generator: it answers a typed question with a probability for each
possible answer. It received the same task as the other models, posed as
a question whose five answer levels are the five anchors of the
sentiment scale, worded as in the prompts below and in the language of
each condition. Its score is the probability-weighted average of the
five levels, mapped onto the -1 to 1 scale. This lineup updates an
initial 2024-2025 evaluation of 11 comparable models with an identical
design. The smallest model, Llama 3.2 1B, rarely followed the
instruction to answer with a number: about one call in 23 returned a
parseable score, and even after retries it scored only 58 to 74 of the
200 sentences, depending on the condition. It was retained to mark the
lower end of the size range, but its metrics rest on a reduced and
non-random subset of sentences and are reported separately.

Each model was tested under three conditions: French prompts on French
text (FR→FR), English prompts on French text (EN→FR), and English
prompts on the machine-translated English text (EN→EN).

All prompts followed the same structure: a task definition, a five-point
sentiment scale from -1.0 to 1.0 with anchor descriptions and
intermediate values allowed, an instruction to consider cultural and
linguistic nuance, and a constraint to respond with a numerical value
only. French prompts were direct translations of the English prompts.

The generative models were queried through the \texttt{ellmer} R

Mistral Large 4 were accessed through OpenRouter. Responses were capped
at 400 tokens; no temperature or seed was set, so each provider's

several providers at different numerical precisions. Each model accessed
through OpenRouter was therefore pinned to a single named provider
endpoint, at the highest precision offered, with fallback to other
providers disabled.\footnote{Endpoint pins (Mistral Large 4 is served
  only by Mistral itself), and the reasoning settings used to suppress
  chain-of-thought in models that reason by default, are listed in
  \texttt{src/94\_models\_map.R}. Mistral Large 4 reasons by default,
  exhausts the 400-token cap, and returns no answer. Its reasoning was
  disabled, as for Qwen3 32B and DeepSeek V3.2.} Gemini 3.5 Flash was

reasoning tokens before answering: 354 output tokens per call on
average, against 7 for Claude Haiku 4.5. Jev was queried through

score. The runs agreed closely: across the 42 model-condition

median of 0.91, from 0.41 for Llama 3.2 3B to 0.97 for GPT-5.6 Luna and
Qwen3 235B-A22B, and Jev, whose output is a probability distribution,
was fully deterministic. Despite explicit instructions to respond with a
bare number, the generative models frequently returned formatted text,
such as ``Sentiment: 0.5''. These responses were parsed with a
pattern-matching function, and only values within the -1.0 to 1.0 range
were retained. Jev's answers needed no parsing.

To check whether the sentence-level findings hold at the scale of a real
corpus, the best-performing model on the benchmark, GPT-5.6 Luna, also
scored all 2,683 full articles under the FR→FR and EN→FR conditions,

the corpus check a second model of a different kind.\footnote{See

  The two GPT-5.6 Luna passes required 5,366 API calls, 7.1 million
  input tokens and 187,000 output tokens, for USD 1.64 at list prices.}
These scores are compared with the French Lexicoder dictionary in the
Results (Figure~\ref{fig-corpus}).

\subsection{Validation and Performance
Evaluation}\label{validation-and-performance-evaluation}

Performance was assessed with four metrics: Pearson correlation between
each model's continuous predictions and the ground truth; mean absolute
error (MAE); weighted F1 on a 7-category classification, from ``very
negative'' to ``very positive'', with cutpoints at −0.66, −0.33, 0.33
and 0.66 and exactly 0 as neutral; and weighted F1 on a 3-category
classification (negative, neutral, positive) that groups the 7
categories into polarity bands. Weighted rather than macro F1 is used
because the manually coded categories are unevenly distributed.
``Somewhat positive'' alone accounts for 44\% of annotated sentences,
while ``very negative'' and ``very positive'' each account for 1\% or
less. Comparing the two classification schemes separates fine-grained
discrimination (7 categories) from polarity classification (3
categories). All metrics were calculated on complete cases only.

Condition comparisons are paired by model and map onto the hypotheses.
The prompt-language hypotheses compare FR→FR with EN→FR, which hold the
French text constant. The translation hypotheses compare EN→FR with
EN→EN, which hold the English prompt constant and isolate the effect of
analyzing the translation. The comparison of FR→FR with EN→EN contrasts
the two complete workflows a researcher might adopt. A non-significant
t-test cannot by itself establish the absence of an effect. Alongside
paired t-tests, two one-sided tests (TOST) therefore assess whether a
difference is small enough to be treated as negligible, with an
equivalence bound of ±0.05 on each metric's scale
(\citeproc{ref-lakensEquivalenceTestsPractical2017}{Lakens, 2017}). The
bound is anchored in the study's own coders: the correlation between one
coder and the mean of the other two ranges from 0.72 to 0.78, a spread
of 0.055, and a difference between conditions smaller than the
disagreement among the human coders is not one worth acting
on.\footnote{The verdicts are sensitive to the bound in the expected
  direction. At ±0.03, only the MAE contrasts and one F1 contrast remain
  equivalent; at ±0.10, every contrast becomes equivalent, including the
  translation contrasts that the paired t-tests find significant. The
  twelve tests are reported without correction for multiple comparisons,
  since each answers a distinct question.} These comparisons and the
averages reported in the Results cover the fourteen models that scored
all 200 sentences (42 model-condition combinations). They exclude the
dictionary baselines, which have no prompt, and Llama 3.2 1B, which
scored a different partial subset of sentences in each condition.

\section{Results}\label{results}

\subsection{Prompt Language (H1a
vs.~H1b)}\label{prompt-language-h1a-vs.-h1b}

Holding the text constant, prompt language made no detectable difference
(Figure~\ref{fig-performance} shows the three conditions for each
model). French prompts on French text (FR→FR) scored slightly higher
than English prompts on the same text (EN→FR) on every metric: mean
correlation 0.708 vs.~0.687, MAE 0.208 vs.~0.216, 3-category F1 0.674
vs.~0.656, and 7-category F1 0.381 vs.~0.363. None of these paired
differences was statistically significant (all \emph{p} \textgreater{}
0.10). Equivalence tests go further. FR→FR and EN→FR were statistically
equivalent within ±0.05 on MAE (\emph{p} \textless{} 0.001), 7-category
F1 (\emph{p} = 0.015), and 3-category F1 (\emph{p} = 0.006). Correlation
was the one metric on which equivalence could not be established. The
90\% confidence interval of the difference, {[}−0.024, 0.066{]}, leaves
room for a French-prompt advantage of up to 0.066, but not for an
English-prompt advantage larger than 0.024. The results contradict H1a
and support H1b on three of four metrics.

The result holds at the scale of the corpus. Figure~\ref{fig-corpus}
plots, for each of the 2,671 full articles with all three scores, the
score GPT-5.6 Luna gave under the English prompt against the score it
gave under the French prompt (left), and the French Lexicoder score
against the French-prompt score (right). The two prompt languages agree
almost perfectly (r = 0.97; 94\% of articles received the same sign),
and Jev shows the same on the same articles (r = 0.997; 98\% the same
sign). The dictionary and the model rank the articles very differently
(r = 0.36 across articles, 0.42 across monthly means), and on 27\% of
articles they disagree even on the sign.\footnote{Correlation ignores
  differences of scale, so this is a disagreement about which articles
  are more positive than others, not about units.} The comparison cannot
say which of the two is right, since neither is the ground truth; the
benchmark results below can.

\subsection{Machine Translation (H2a
vs.~H2b)}\label{machine-translation-h2a-vs.-h2b}

For the LLMs, translation carried a cost. Holding the English prompt
constant, analyzing the translation instead of the original (EN→EN
vs.~EN→FR) lowered 3-category F1 by 0.035 (0.621 vs.~0.656, \emph{p} =
0.004). The accompanying drop in correlation (0.659 vs.~0.687) was not
statistically significant (\emph{p} = 0.12). Differences in MAE and
7-category F1 remained within the ±0.05 equivalence bound. Compared with
the fully French workflow (FR→FR), the fully English workflow (EN→EN)
lowered correlation by 0.048 (\emph{p} = 0.002) and 3-category F1 by
0.053 (\emph{p} \textless{} 0.001).

The dictionaries showed the opposite pattern. The English Lexicoder
applied to the translation correlated with human judgments at r = 0.548,
against 0.357 for the French Lexicoder applied to the original. The
difference is 0.191 (95\% bootstrap CI {[}0.059, 0.326{]}). The English
dictionary also reached a higher 3-category F1 (0.588 vs.~0.557). The
two dictionaries also differ in construction, so this contrast does not
isolate language. Its direction nonetheless matches the bridge view. For
the LLMs, the results support H2b: translation lowered polarity
classification whichever prompt was held constant, and it lowered
correlation when the two complete workflows are compared.

\subsection{Viability Against Dictionary Methods
(RQ2)}\label{viability-against-dictionary-methods-rq2}

General-purpose LLMs, used without fine-tuning, correlated with human
judgments well above both dictionaries (Figure~\ref{fig-performance};
exact values and confidence intervals in Table \ref{tbl:performance}).
The best configuration, GPT-5.6 Luna with French prompts, reached r =
0.81. Jev came close behind the leaders, with r = 0.77 in both
conditions on French text and the second-highest 7-category F1 (0.528 in
EN→FR). Twelve of the 42 model-condition combinations correlated with
the ground truth more strongly than individual human coders agreed with
the mean of the other two (mean leave-one-out r = 0.748, range
0.724--0.779).\footnote{This comparison slightly favors the models,
  because the ground truth they are compared against averages three
  coders rather than two. Agreement between annotators is not a strict
  ceiling on machine performance
  (\citeproc{ref-richieInterannotatorAgreementNot2022}{Richie et al.,
  2022}).}

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

\subsection{Prompt Language: Multilingual Competence, Within Scope
Conditions}\label{prompt-language-multilingual-competence-within-scope-conditions}

For French and current models, the results side with the
multilingual-competence view. English prompts brought no advantage, and
equivalence tests ruled out any English-prompt gain larger than a few
hundredths, even on correlation, where strict equivalence could not be
established. The equivalence held at the scale of the full corpus
(Figure~\ref{fig-corpus}). Earlier social science evidence showed that
labels agree across prompt languages
(\citeproc{ref-rathjeGPTEffectiveTool2024}{Rathje et al., 2024}). This
study extends that finding to the accuracy of the labels against human
judgment. It is also consistent with the minimal differences observed
for the strongest model in Arabic
(\citeproc{ref-Kmainasi2025Native}{Kmainasi et al., 2025}). The results
do not refute the pivot-language evidence so much as delimit it. That
evidence comes largely from reasoning and inference benchmarks, older or
smaller models, and lower-resourced languages
(\citeproc{ref-laiChatGPTEnglishComprehensive2023}{Lai et al., 2023};
\citeproc{ref-Lin2022Few}{X. V. Lin et al., 2022};
\citeproc{ref-Muennighoff2023Crosslingual}{Muennighoff et al., 2023}).
The representational bias toward English that it points to
(\citeproc{ref-wendler_etal24}{Wendler et al., 2024}) need not produce
worse instruction-following for a simple judgment in a language as well
represented as French. The smaller open-weight models behaved
differently. Llama 3.2 3B correlated with human judgments at r = 0.48
with French prompts but 0.13 with English ones. This suggests that
sensitivity to prompt language may reappear below a certain model size,
although a single model cannot establish this.

Prompt language does carry a cost that the accuracy results do not show.
The French prompt is 6\% longer than the English one in characters, yet
it cost 10\% to 34\% more input tokens, depending on the model
(Table~\ref{tbl-tokens}).\footnote{Jev's input is TypeSafe's structured
  question rather than the prompt shown above, so its token counts do
  not measure the same text. It is left out of the tokenizer comparisons
  in this paragraph.} The difference is a property of each model's
tokenizer, not of its size or license. Vocabularies that favor English
encode English text with similar efficiency across models (1.17 to 1.25
tokens per word) but split French words into more pieces (1.38 to 1.87
tokens per word). Ahia et al.
(\citeproc{ref-ahiaAllLanguagesCost2023}{2023}) document the same
disparity across many languages. Mistral's claim of a tokenizer that is
cheaper on French (\citeproc{ref-mistralaiMistralNeMo2024}{Mistral AI,
2024}) held for one of its two models. Mistral Large 4 encoded French
more compactly than any other model and had the smallest French-prompt
overhead (10\%). Mistral Small 3.2 did not stand out (1.45 tokens per
French word, a 15\% overhead). Output length did not differ by prompt
language. Since accuracy was equivalent, the English prompt is the
cheaper of two equally good options. The saving per 1,000 sentences
ranges from under 6\% for models whose output tokens weigh most in the
bill to 10\% to 31\% for models billed mostly on input. Translating the
text first would save a few percent more, at the accuracy cost reported
above.

\subsection{Translation: A Bridge for Dictionaries, a Loss for
LLMs}\label{translation-a-bridge-for-dictionaries-a-loss-for-llms}

The translation results are the article's main contribution to the
debate. The same machine translation that improved the dictionary
lowered the LLMs' performance. This divergence reconciles the bridge and
loss views rather than choosing between them. Translation helps tools
that cannot read the source language, and it costs information for tools
that can. The dictionary result is consistent with a long line of
evidence that translating into English lets researchers use
better-validated English resources
(\citeproc{ref-devriesNoLongerLost2018}{De Vries et al., 2018};
\citeproc{ref-prokschMultilingualSentimentAnalysis2019}{Proksch et al.,
2019}). LLMs already read French. For them, translation offers no such
gain, and it exposes the analysis to the alterations of affective
meaning that translation introduces
(\citeproc{ref-Mohammad2016translation}{Mohammad et al., 2016}). The
same pattern appears, in passing, in the largest LLM study of party
manifestos to date: inserting a machine-translation step before the
analysis of 15 non-English manifestos lowered the correlation with
expert surveys, and the authors dropped the step from their final design
(\citeproc{ref-benoitUsingLargeLanguage2026}{Benoit et al., 2026}). The
present study makes that incidental observation the object of a formal
test. The finding runs against evidence that translating inputs into
English improves multilingual models
(\citeproc{ref-Etxaniz2024Multilingual}{Etxaniz et al., 2024}). That
evidence comes from benchmarks translated out of English, whereas the
sentences analyzed here were written in French.

\subsection{Viability: Optimism, With
Validation}\label{viability-optimism-with-validation}

On RQ2, the results side with the optimistic literature
(\citeproc{ref-gilardiChatGPTOutperformsCrowd2023}{Gilardi et al.,
2023}; \citeproc{ref-rathjeGPTEffectiveTool2024}{Rathje et al., 2024};
\citeproc{ref-tornbergLargeLanguageModels2025}{T"ornberg, 2025}) and
extend it in two directions. First, the advantage of LLMs over
dictionaries holds against a native-language dictionary as well as a
translated one. Second, open-weight models were viable from about 8B
parameters, and the largest approached the best closed-weight models.
This is consistent with evidence from German
(\citeproc{ref-klahnDictionariesLLMsEvaluation2025}{Klähn et al.,
2025}). Below that size, performance became erratic, and a 1B-parameter
model could not follow the task. For researchers weighing the
reproducibility concerns raised by proprietary models
(\citeproc{ref-spirlingWhyOpensourceGenerative2023}{Spirling, 2023}),
this makes a credible open-weight option available at low cost. Qwen3
235B-A22B and GPT-OSS 120B reached 90--95\% of the best closed-weight
correlation for USD 0.02--0.03 per thousand sentences. Models from a
French developer showed no French advantage. Neither Mistral model did
notably better with French prompts, and both trailed the leading
closed-weight models. A different kind of model also proved viable. Jev,
which returns a probability for each point of the scale rather than
generated text, came within 0.04 of the best correlation for about USD
0.02 per thousand sentences. For French text, the answer to RQ2 is
therefore yes. A general-purpose LLM measures sentiment better than the
Lexicoder dictionary on every criterion that rewards discrimination, and
the cost of running one is no longer a reason to prefer the dictionary.
This is the condition under which comparative research in a non-English
language becomes cheap: no translation step, no training data, no
fine-tuning, a validated model, and a prompt in whichever language the
researcher writes most easily.

The cautious literature is not dismissed. Performance varied widely
across models, which reinforces the need to validate each model on each
task (\citeproc{ref-pangakisAutomatedAnnotationGenerative2023}{Pangakis
et al., 2023}). Two further results qualify the picture. First, the
dictionaries' near-zero output gave them a slightly lower MAE than the
LLMs. MAE alone can thus reward a method that barely discriminates.
Second, all models performed markedly worse on 7-category classification
than on 3-category polarity. The two results fit together once one
separates ranking sentences from placing them on the scale. LLMs order
sentences and reproduce the spread of human ratings. Their correlation
with the ground truth far exceeds that of the dictionaries. From 8B
parameters upward, their scores spread at least as widely as the human
ratings (SD 0.30 to 0.49 vs.~0.286), whereas the dictionaries' scores
cluster near zero (SD = 0.065 for the English dictionary). What LLMs do
poorly is place scores on the right side of fixed category thresholds.
They overstate extremes: 10.6\% of LLM scores exceed ±0.66, against
1.5\% of ground-truth values. A sentence rated 0.5 by humans and 0.8 by
a model is correctly ordered but misclassified. The ground truth
contributes to the mismatch, since averaging three coders pulls it
toward the center. Gradations in sentiment are one of the main arguments
for dictionaries
(\citeproc{ref-vanderveenAdvantagesLexiconbasedSentiment2025}{Van Der
Veen \& Bleich, 2025}). In this corpus, a single off-the-shelf
dictionary captured them less well than the LLMs. The gap does not close
with aggregation: on the full corpus, the dictionary agreed only weakly
with the model, by article and by month (Figure~\ref{fig-corpus}). What
the dictionary retains is transparency, since every score can be traced
to the words that produced it, and stability over time, since a
dictionary is not deprecated. These make it a useful baseline and
robustness check alongside an LLM. They are not a reason to prefer it as
the primary measure.

\section{Limitations}\label{limitations}

Several limitations qualify these findings. The benchmark is modest in
size and reliability. Its 200 sentences meet common size
recommendations, but agreement between individual coders was moderate
(Krippendorff's alpha = 0.597). Simulations show that such conditions
raise the risk of misjudging automated methods
(\citeproc{ref-songValidationsWeTrust2020}{Song et al., 2020}). The
coders were members of the research team rather than external
annotators. The benchmark is also made of sentences, whereas the
Lexicoder dictionaries were built and validated for the tone of whole
articles, where word counts are larger and more stable; the
sentence-level comparison therefore favors the LLMs, and the
corpus-level comparison cannot settle the matter because it has no human
ground truth at the article level. An article-level validation is the
natural next step. Only two sentences fall in the very negative category
and one in the very positive, so class-level F1 scores for these
categories are unstable. The findings concern French only. The corpus is
French-language news on a single domain, open-source software. Neither
the absence of a prompt-language effect nor the cost of translation can
be assumed to extend to other languages or genres.

Methodologically, the EN→EN condition relies on Google Translate, and
translation quality was deliberately not validated. The condition
reproduces what a researcher who does not read the source language can
do: translate automatically and analyze the result. Its lower
performance should be read as the cost of that workflow with this
translation system rather than of translation as such; a translation
produced by an LLM might narrow the gap. The articles are public news
texts from 1995 to 2024 and may have been part of the models' training
data. This could inflate absolute performance, but it does not affect
the comparisons between conditions, which rest on the same sentences. A
single standardized prompt was used across the generative models for
comparability. This may understate the performance achievable with
model-specific prompting. Jev received the same scale in a different
format, and its score is a probability-weighted average, which could in
principle pull scores toward the middle of the scale and flatter its
MAE. In practice its scores spread as widely as those of the best
generative models (SD 0.36 against 0.37 for GPT-5.6 Luna; 7\% of scores
beyond ±0.66 against 6\% to 11\%), so its MAE is not an artefact of a
compressed scale. Mistral Large 4 was evaluated as a preview release,
which may change before its general availability. The models tested do
not include fine-tuned or domain-specific classifiers, which can rival
general-purpose LLMs
(\citeproc{ref-mochtakParlasentMappingSentiment2025}{Mochtak et al.,
2025}). Costs are reported at list prices and count cached prompt tokens
at the full input rate, so they are upper bounds. Finally, this
evaluation is a snapshot. Several models from the earlier version of
this study, including GPT-4o and Gemini 2.0 Flash, were deprecated by
their providers within eighteen months, and the specific rankings should
be expected to age quickly.

\section{Conclusion}\label{conclusion}

Researchers analyzing French text with LLMs can set aside both inherited
reflexes. The language of the prompt is a matter of convenience. English
instructions brought no advantage, and equivalence tests rule out any
substantial one. The language of the text is not a matter of
convenience. Translating into English improved the dictionary but
lowered the LLMs' performance. The comparative strategy that served the
dictionary era does not transfer to models that read the source
language.

Two questions follow. The first is whether these results hold beyond
French, particularly for languages less represented in training data,
where the case for English prompts and translation is strongest. The
design ports directly to such tests: the same three conditions, a
manually annotated benchmark, and a different target language. The
second is whether the size threshold observed here, below which
open-weight models become erratic and prompt-sensitive, is a stable
feature of current models.

The practical answer is equally clear, and it matters most to the
researchers the English-centered era left aside: those who work in
another language, have no annotated training set, and cannot train or
fine-tune a model. For them the choice is between an off-the-shelf
dictionary and an off-the-shelf model, and the model wins. A
general-purpose LLM, validated on a manually annotated sample, measures
sentiment better than the Lexicoder dictionary on every criterion but
transparency, and open-weight models from about 8B parameters do so for
a few cents per thousand sentences. The dictionary remains a baseline
and a robustness check. For researchers working with French text, the
evidence settles three points: use a validated LLM, prompt in whichever
language is convenient, and analyze the text in the language in which it
was written.
