\title{Language Doesn't Matter: Evaluating the Efficacy of Large
Language Models in Analyzing Sentiment in Non-English Textual Data}


\shorttitle{Language Doesn't Matter}


\usepackage{etoolbox}








\authorsnames{Laurence-Olivier M. Foisy,Camille Pelletier,Étienne
Proulx,Yannick Dufresne}





\affiliation{
{Département de science politique, Université Laval}}




\leftheader{Foisy, Pelletier, Proulx and Dufresne}

\date{2026-09-16}


\abstract{This study examines whether general-purpose large language
models (LLMs) can analyze sentiment in non-English text without
task-specific fine-tuning, and whether prompt language affects their
performance. We evaluate twelve open- and closed-weight LLMs on 200
French sentences drawn from a corpus of 2,683 French news articles on
open-source software, comparing performance to dictionary-based methods
across three conditions: French prompts on French text, English prompts
on French text, and English prompts on English translations. Prompt
language has no statistically meaningful effect on performance, and
equivalence tests rule out any substantial English-prompting advantage,
contradicting prior evidence of a cross-linguistic English-prompting
advantage; translating the text into English, by contrast, lowers
performance. Leading closed-weight models achieve strong performance (r
\textgreater{} 0.80), larger open-weight models offer a viable, more
accessible alternative, and the smallest model tested (1B parameters)
fails to follow the task instructions. LLMs outperform a standard
sentiment dictionary on correlation with human judgments and on coarse
classification, though the dictionary offers greater transparency. All
models perform substantially better on three-category
(positive/neutral/negative) than on seven-category classification. These
findings suggest social science researchers can use general-purpose LLMs
for non-English sentiment analysis in whichever prompt language is most
convenient, without sacrificing accuracy. }

\keywords{Sentiment Analysis, Large Language models, Cross-lingual
NLP, French Language, Open-Source models, Computational Social
Science, Prompt Engineering}

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Most foundational large language models (LLMs) are trained predominantly
on English data (\citeproc{ref-touvron_etal23a}{Touvron et al., 2023}),
and a growing body of research reports that prompting in English
improves performance on non-English tasks, even when the target text
itself is non-English (\citeproc{ref-Jiang2020How}{Jiang et al., 2020};
\citeproc{ref-Lin2022Few}{Lin et al., 2022};
\citeproc{ref-Muennighoff2023Crosslingual}{Muennighoff et al., 2023};
\citeproc{ref-Nie2024Decomposed}{Nie et al., 2024}). If this ``English
prompt advantage'' holds generally, it imposes a real cost on
non-anglophone researchers, who would need to translate their prompts to
get the best results from otherwise capable models.

This paper tests that assumption directly, using sentiment analysis of
French-language text as a case study: \textbf{does the language of the
prompt meaningfully affect an LLM's performance, or does it not matter?}
Sentiment analysis is well suited to this test because it demands
cultural and linguistic nuance beyond simple translation, it is a common
analytical task across the social sciences, it admits both continuous
(intensity) and categorical (polarity) evaluation, and established
dictionary-based methods offer a meaningful performance benchmark.

Answering this question well requires first establishing that
general-purpose LLMs --- used off the shelf, without task-specific
fine-tuning --- can do sentiment analysis on non-English text
competently at all. Specialized models such as BERT variants achieve
strong performance through fine-tuning
(\citeproc{ref-Anirban2021Sentiment}{Anirban \& Abhik, 2021};
\citeproc{ref-Michailidis2024Comparative}{Michailidis, 2024};
\citeproc{ref-Rafeef2023Evaluation}{Rafeef A. Hameed et al., 2023}), but
require machine learning expertise, computational resources, and labeled
data that many social science researchers lack
(\citeproc{ref-Chalkidis2020LEGAL}{Chalkidis et al., 2020};
\citeproc{ref-Laurer2023Less}{Laurer et al., 2023};
\citeproc{ref-Strubell2019Energy}{Strubell et al., 2019}).
General-purpose LLMs accessed through APIs offer a more accessible
alternative, requiring primarily prompting skills
(\citeproc{ref-Ghosh2023Navigating}{Ghosh et al., 2023};
\citeproc{ref-Perron2024Demystifying}{Perron et al., 2024}) --- but only
if they perform adequately without fine-tuning in the first place.

We therefore ask two questions: (1) does prompt language (French
vs.~English) meaningfully affect sentiment analysis performance on
French text, and (2) can general-purpose LLMs --- open and
closed-weight\footnote{While ``open source'' traditionally implies
  access aligned with FOSS principles, its usage within the LLM context
  often refers more specifically to the public release of model weights,
  a condition more accurately described as ``open weights.'' This paper
  adopts this common LLM terminology while acknowledging that most
  models in our ``open source'' category are technically ``open
  weights.''} --- perform this task well enough, without fine-tuning, to
be a viable alternative to dictionary-based methods for social science
research. We evaluate leading open and closed-source foundational LLMs
on French news text and find that prompt language has no meaningful
effect on performance, and that several models --- including open-weight
ones --- perform competitively with or better than traditional
dictionary methods.

\section{Literature Review}\label{literature-review}

Specialized, fine-tuned models achieve strong multilingual sentiment
analysis performance --- often above 85-95\% accuracy across
Indo-European, Semitic, and Asian languages
(\citeproc{ref-Ahmadian2024Hybrid}{Ahmadian et al., 2024};
\citeproc{ref-Anirban2021Sentiment}{Anirban \& Abhik, 2021};
\citeproc{ref-ElJundi2019hULMonA}{ElJundi et al., 2019};
\citeproc{ref-Michailidis2024Comparative}{Michailidis, 2024};
\citeproc{ref-Rafeef2023Evaluation}{Rafeef A. Hameed et al., 2023}),
including for lower-resource languages
(\citeproc{ref-Nazir2025Leveraging}{Nazir et al., 2025};
\citeproc{ref-Salahudeen2023HausaNLP}{Salahudeen et al., 2023};
\citeproc{ref-Tela2024Transferring}{Tela et al., 2024}) and via
cross-lingual transfer (\citeproc{ref-Kumar2021Sentiment}{Kumar \&
Albuquerque, 2021}; \citeproc{ref-Pan2023Evaluation}{Pan et al., 2023}).
This performance is costly: such models require large parameter counts
(\citeproc{ref-Francesco2021XLM}{Francesco et al., 2021};
\citeproc{ref-Rafeef2023Evaluation}{Rafeef A. Hameed et al., 2023}),
fine-tuning expertise (\citeproc{ref-Kotelnikova2023Cross}{Kotelnikova
et al., 2023}; \citeproc{ref-Priban2024comparative}{Přibáň et al.,
2024}), and substantial labeled training data
(\citeproc{ref-Mikhail2024Multilingual}{Mikhail et al., 2024};
\citeproc{ref-Salahudeen2023HausaNLP}{Salahudeen et al., 2023}) ---
barriers that put them out of reach for many social science researchers.

By contrast, general-purpose foundational LLMs used without fine-tuning
remain understudied for multilingual sentiment analysis. Preliminary
work with Mistral-7B, LLaMA2, and instruction-tuned models suggests
promise in Italian, Russian, and cross-lingual settings
(\citeproc{ref-Buscemi2024ChatGPT}{Buscemi \& Proverbio, 2024};
\citeproc{ref-Plagwitz2024Zero}{Plagwitz et al., 2024};
\citeproc{ref-Rusnachenko2024Large}{Rusnachenko et al., 2024};
\citeproc{ref-Venerito2024Large}{Venerito \& Iannone, 2024}). Evidence
on prompt language effects is mixed: some studies find language-specific
prompting advantages (\citeproc{ref-Hamalainen2022Video}{Hämäläinen et
al., 2022}; \citeproc{ref-Vileikyte2024Sentiment}{Vileikytė et al.,
2024}), while others report that English prompting broadly improves
cross-linguistic performance (\citeproc{ref-Jiang2020How}{Jiang et al.,
2020}; \citeproc{ref-Lin2022Few}{Lin et al., 2022};
\citeproc{ref-Muennighoff2023Crosslingual}{Muennighoff et al., 2023};
\citeproc{ref-Nie2024Decomposed}{Nie et al., 2024}). This study
addresses this gap by evaluating leading open- and closed-weight
foundational LLMs on French text.

\section{Data \& Methods}\label{data-methods}

This study evaluates general-purpose large language models against
dictionary-based baselines for sentiment analysis of French text,
testing both prompt-language effects and overall LLM viability without
task-specific fine-tuning.

\subsection{Translation}\label{translation}

To enable the EN→EN condition described below, we translated the French
corpus into English using Google Translate's document translation
service, processed in batches via an automated R workflow that preserved
document IDs to keep French and English versions aligned in the final
dataset.\footnote{See \texttt{src/21\_translate\_to\_english.R} in the
  project repository for the full translation pipeline.}

\subsection{Manual Annotation}\label{manual-annotation}

We created a validation benchmark of 200 sentences randomly sampled from
the corpus, each translated into English via Google Translate and the
\texttt{polyglotR} package. Three independent coders rated each
sentence's sentiment on a scale from -1 (very negative) to 1 (very
positive) using a custom Shiny application with keyboard shortcuts and
scale reminders; we averaged the three ratings per sentence to obtain
the ground truth used to evaluate both dictionary-based and LLM methods.

\subsection{Dictionary-Based Sentiment
Analysis}\label{dictionary-based-sentiment-analysis}

We applied lexicon-based sentiment scoring to both the French original
and English translation as a classical baseline. For French text, we
used a customized version of the French Lexicoder Sentiment Dictionary
(\texttt{frlsd}) (\citeproc{ref-duval_petry16}{Duval \& Pétry, 2016}) on
lowercased, stopword-removed, sentence-segmented text, scoring each
sentence as (positive terms − negative terms) / total words. For English
translations, we used the standard Lexicoder Sentiment Dictionary
(\citeproc{ref-young_andsoroka12}{Young \& and Soroka, 2012}) with
additional handling for contractions, proper nouns, and negation (e.g.,
``not good'' scored as negative), scoring each sentence as ((positive +
negated\_negative) − (negative + negated\_positive)) / total words.

\subsection{LLM-Based Sentiment
Analysis}\label{llm-based-sentiment-analysis}

We evaluated twelve general-purpose foundational LLMs, selected for
prominence and affordability --- realistic choices for researchers who
need to analyze large corpora rather than a single sample. Specialized
fine-tuned models can outperform them on narrow tasks, at the cost in
expertise and infrastructure set out above. Tooling adds a further
barrier that is rarely acknowledged: to our knowledge no R package
exposes a fine-tuning workflow, so a researcher working in the language
most common in quantitative social science must write and maintain
training code themselves, whereas general-purpose models are reachable
through an API with no comparable setup
(\citeproc{ref-Ghosh2023Navigating}{Ghosh et al., 2023};
\citeproc{ref-Perron2024Demystifying}{Perron et al., 2024}).

The evaluation spans nine open-weight models --- Llama 3.2 1B, Llama 3.2
3B, Llama 3.1 8B, GPT-OSS 20B, Qwen3 32B, Llama 4 Scout, GPT-OSS 120B,
Qwen3 235B-A22B, and DeepSeek V3.2, ranging from 1B to 671B total
parameters --- and three closed-weight models: Claude Haiku 4.5
(Anthropic), Gemini 3.5 Flash (Google), and GPT-5.6 Luna (OpenAI). This
lineup updates an initial 2024-2025 evaluation of 11 comparable models
using an identical experimental design; several models from that
original evaluation (e.g., GPT-4o, Gemini 2.0 Flash) had already been
deprecated by their providers within eighteen months, underscoring how
quickly this landscape moves. The smallest model, Llama 3.2 1B, rarely
followed the instruction to answer with a number: about one call in 23
returned a parseable score, and even after retries it produced a usable
score for only 58 to 74 of the 200 sentences, depending on the
condition. We retain it deliberately: while models of a few billion
parameters can handle the task, a 1B-parameter model appears too small
to follow the instructions reliably at all, which marks a practical
lower bound on model size. Its metrics rest on a reduced and non-random
subset of sentences and are not directly comparable to those of the
other models.

For each model, we tested three conditions to isolate the effects of
prompt language and text language: French prompts on French text
(FR→FR), English prompts on French text (EN→FR), and English prompts on
the English translation (EN→EN).

All prompts followed a standardized structure: a task definition, a
five-point sentiment scale from -1.0 to 1.0 with anchor descriptions
(allowing intermediate values), an instruction to consider cultural and
linguistic nuance, and a constraint to respond with only a numerical
value. French prompts were direct translations of the English prompts.

We queried each model via the \texttt{ellmer} R package, using the
vendors' own APIs for the three closed-weight models and OpenRouter for
the nine open-weight models, with responses capped at 400 tokens.
Because OpenRouter can serve the same model through several providers at
different numerical precisions, each open-weight model was pinned to a
single named provider endpoint, at the highest precision offered, with
fallback to other providers disabled.\footnote{Endpoint pins, and the
  reasoning settings used to suppress chain-of-thought in models that
  reason by default, are listed in \texttt{src/94\_models\_map.R}.} Each
sentence was scored three times per model-condition combination (21,600
scores in total), and we took the mean of the three runs as the final
score. Despite explicit instructions to respond with a bare number,
models frequently returned formatted text (e.g., ``Sentiment: 0.5''); we
parsed these with a pattern-matching function validated against the
expected -1.0 to 1.0 range.

\subsection{Validation and Performance
Evaluation}\label{validation-and-performance-evaluation}

We assessed performance with three complementary metrics: Pearson
correlation between each model's continuous predictions and the manually
annotated ground truth; weighted F1 score on a 7-category classification
(``very\_negative'' through ``very\_positive''); and weighted F1 score
on a simplified 3-category classification (negative/neutral/positive)
that groups the 7 categories into broad polarity bands. We use weighted
(rather than macro) F1 because the manually coded categories are
unevenly distributed --- ``somewhat\_positive'' alone accounts for 44\%
of annotated sentences, while ``very\_negative'' and ``very\_positive''
each account for 1\% or less --- so a weighted average better reflects
overall classification quality given this class imbalance. Comparing the
two classification schemes lets us separately assess fine-grained
sentiment discrimination (7-category) and broad polarity classification
(3-category). We additionally report mean absolute error (MAE) between
continuous predictions and the ground truth. All metrics were calculated
on complete cases only.

\section{Results}\label{results}

We evaluated models using the three metrics described above,
complemented by mean absolute error (MAE). The condition analysis and
the averages reported below cover the eleven models that scored all 200
sentences (33 model-condition combinations) and exclude the dictionary
baselines, which have no prompt. Llama 3.2 1B is reported separately: it
scored a different, partial subset of sentences in each condition, so
its differences across conditions would compare unlike samples.

\subsection{Prompt Language Has No Meaningful Effect; Translating the
Text
Does}\label{prompt-language-has-no-meaningful-effect-translating-the-text-does}

Holding the text constant, prompt language made no detectable
difference. French prompts on French text (FR→FR) edged out English
prompts on the same text (EN→FR) on every metric --- mean correlation
0.700 vs.~0.673, MAE 0.209 vs.~0.217, 3-category F1 0.669 vs.~0.646,
7-category F1 0.364 vs.~0.347 --- but none of these paired differences
was statistically significant (all \emph{p} \textgreater{} 0.12).
Because a non-significant test cannot on its own establish the absence
of an effect, we also ran two one-sided tests (TOST) against an
equivalence bound of ±0.05 on each metric's scale. FR→FR and EN→FR were
statistically equivalent on MAE (\emph{p} = 0.002), 7-category F1
(\emph{p} = 0.034), and 3-category F1 (\emph{p} = 0.034). Correlation
was the one metric where equivalence could not be established: the 90\%
confidence interval of the difference, {[}−0.032, 0.085{]}, leaves room
for a French-prompt advantage of up to 0.085, but not for an
English-prompt advantage larger than 0.032.

Translating the text into English, by contrast, had a measurable cost.
Relative to FR→FR, scoring the English translation (EN→EN) lowered mean
correlation by 0.053 (0.700 vs.~0.647, \emph{p} = 0.007) and 3-category
F1 by 0.063 (0.669 vs.~0.606, \emph{p} \textless{} 0.001); the
3-category loss also held against EN→FR (0.041, \emph{p} = 0.005).
Differences in MAE and 7-category F1 across all three conditions
remained within the ±0.05 equivalence bound. The translation penalty is
therefore concentrated in how well models rank sentences by sentiment
and identify their polarity, consistent with information lost in machine
translation.

\subsection{LLMs Are a Viable Alternative to Dictionary
Methods}\label{llms-are-a-viable-alternative-to-dictionary-methods}

General-purpose LLMs, used without fine-tuning, correlated with human
judgments well above the dictionary baselines. The best configurations
exceeded r = 0.80: GPT-5.6 Luna led on correlation (r = 0.810 in FR→FR),
Claude Haiku 4.5 on both 3-category F1 (0.778) and 7-category F1 (0.537,
both FR→FR), and Gemini 3.5 Flash on MAE (0.156 in EN→FR). Nine of the
33 model-condition combinations correlated with the ground truth more
strongly than individual human coders agreed with the mean of the other
two (mean leave-one-out r = 0.748, range 0.724--0.779).\footnote{This
  comparison favors the models slightly, since the ground truth they are
  compared against averages three coders rather than two.}

Closed-weight models outperformed open-weight ones on average (mean r =

the top of the open-weight range: Qwen3 235B-A22B reached r = 0.774 in

and GPT-OSS 20B averaged r = 0.733 and 0.716 across conditions.
Performance degraded steadily below that. Llama 3.1 8B remained usable
(r = 0.575--0.612), Llama 3.2 3B was erratic (r = 0.476 in FR→FR but
0.132 in EN→FR), and Llama 3.2 1B failed outright: it returned a usable
score for only 58 to 74 sentences per condition, and on those its
correlation with the ground truth ranged from −0.199 to 0.100.

The English Lexicoder Sentiment Dictionary reached a correlation of
0.548 and the French version 0.357, against a mean of 0.673 for the
eleven LLMs; on 3-category F1 the dictionaries (0.588 and 0.557) also
trailed the LLM mean (0.640), though the English dictionary outperformed
Llama 3.2 3B and Qwen3 32B. On MAE, however, both dictionaries (0.203
and 0.210) did slightly better than the LLM average (0.213). This
reflects the dictionaries' compressed output rather than accuracy: their
scores cluster near zero (SD = 0.065 for the English dictionary,
vs.~0.286 for the ground truth and 0.38--0.47 for the LLMs), which keeps
absolute errors small on a sample dominated by mildly positive sentences
while discriminating poorly between them.

\subsection{Classification
Granularity}\label{classification-granularity}

All models performed substantially better on 3-category than 7-category
classification (best F1 of 0.778 vs.~0.537; mean 0.640 vs.~0.349; see
Figure 3, below). Models most reliably identified positive and negative
sentiment (7-category F1 = 0.482 and 0.441, rising to 0.690 and 0.627 in
the 3-category scheme) and struggled most with the extreme categories
(very\_negative F1 = 0.256, very\_positive F1 = 0.124), which are nearly
absent from the sample (two sentences and one sentence respectively).
Neutral remained the hardest 3-category label (F1 = 0.258). Researchers
needing fine-grained sentiment distinctions may find general-purpose
LLMs insufficient without further adaptation; the broader
positive/neutral/negative distinction is handled far more reliably.

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

Our central finding is that prompt language does not meaningfully affect
LLM sentiment analysis performance on French text --- a more nuanced
picture than the English-prompting advantage reported elsewhere
(\citeproc{ref-Jiang2020How}{Jiang et al., 2020};
\citeproc{ref-Lin2022Few}{Lin et al., 2022};
\citeproc{ref-Muennighoff2023Crosslingual}{Muennighoff et al., 2023}).
If anything, the point estimates favored French prompts, and equivalence
tests ruled out any English-prompt advantage larger than a few
hundredths on every metric. What did cost performance was translating
the text itself: scoring English translations lowered correlation and
3-category F1 relative to working with the French original. This
challenges the assumption that researchers must work in English to get
good results: francophone researchers can use their native language with
no analytical penalty, teams can choose prompt language by convenience
rather than performance, and multi-national collaborations can let each
team prompt in its preferred language while keeping results comparable
--- without needing to standardize on English. The practical warning
runs the other way: translating a corpus into English before analysis, a
common step in computational work on non-English text, is not neutral.
Theoretically, this suggests the ``English prompt advantage'' documented
in prior work may be task-dependent, language-pair specific, or
diminishing as models improve.

This finding is only practically useful if LLMs are themselves viable
for the task, and our results support that they are. Leading
closed-weight models achieved strong correlations (GPT-5.6 Luna, r =
0.810) and 3-category F1 scores (Claude Haiku 4.5, 0.778) without any
task-specific fine-tuning, several configurations agreed with the ground
truth more closely than our individual human coders did, and the largest
open-weight models came close to them (Qwen3 235B-A22B, r = 0.774).
Model size sets a floor, however. Models from roughly 8B parameters
upward produced usable scores, a 3B model was unreliable across
conditions, and a 1B model could not follow the instruction to return a
number at all. Together, these results mean social scientists can
confidently deploy leading closed-weight LLMs, and larger open-weight
ones, for French sentiment analysis in whichever prompt language suits
their workflow, without fine-tuning expertise or heavy computational
resources (\citeproc{ref-Priban2024comparative}{Přibáň et al., 2024})
--- provided they avoid the smallest models.

Two secondary results qualify this picture. First, LLMs clearly
outperformed the Lexicoder Sentiment Dictionary on correlation (mean LLM
r = 0.673 vs.~0.548 for the English dictionary) and, more modestly, on
3-category F1 (0.640 vs.~0.588), but the dictionaries' near-zero output
gave them a slightly lower MAE --- a reminder that MAE alone can reward
a method that barely discriminates. A transparent, fully interpretable
dictionary remains a defensible baseline for basic polarity detection,
but not for measuring sentiment intensity. Second, all models performed
markedly worse on fine-grained 7-category classification than on
3-category polarity (mean F1 of 0.349 vs.~0.640), struggling most with
extreme sentiment categories; researchers needing granular sentiment
distinctions should not expect general-purpose LLMs to deliver this out
of the box, though Claude Haiku 4.5's stronger 7-category performance
(0.537) suggests some models handle nuance better than others.

\section{Limitations}\label{limitations}

Several limitations qualify these findings. The 200-sentence validation
sample, though randomly drawn, is a small fraction of the corpus and may
not capture its full linguistic range; the corpus itself is
French-language news on a single technical/political domain (open-source
software), so performance hierarchies and cross-lingual prompting
effects may not extrapolate to other languages, especially those less
represented in LLM training data, or to other genres such as social
media or political debate text with different linguistic styles.

Methodologically, English translations relied on Google Translate
without a formal fidelity check, so translation artifacts could have
affected the EN→EN condition specifically. Model selection prioritized
affordability and prominence over including every top-performing model
globally, reflecting the perspective of researchers who need to analyze
corpora of millions of sentences rather than a few hundred. A single
standardized prompt was used across all models for comparability, which
may understate peak performance achievable through model-specific prompt
engineering; the need for a response-parsing function also indicates
models did not always follow the requested output format exactly.

Finally, no cost or runtime data were collected, though this information
matters for researchers assessing feasibility. More fundamentally, this
evaluation is a snapshot: given how quickly LLM capabilities and
provider offerings change --- several models evaluated in an earlier
version of this study were deprecated within eighteen months --- the
specific rankings reported here should be expected to age quickly, even
as the higher-level finding regarding prompt language is likely to hold
across model generations.

\section{Conclusion}\label{conclusion}

Prompt language does not constrain how social scientists work with
non-English text. The practical consequence runs further than
francophone teams being free to prompt in French: prompt language ceases
to be a design decision that needs defending at all, and the translation
step that has often preceded computational analysis of non-English
corpora loses its rationale.

That opens work this study could not do. Comparative projects spanning
several languages can let each team prompt in its own and pool the
results, without a translation pass that would itself require
validation. Whether this holds beyond French --- particularly for
languages less represented in training data, where the English-prompting
advantage was first reported --- is the natural next test, and this
design ports to it directly: the same three conditions, the same
manually annotated benchmark, a different target language.

Method choice remains a judgment about research goals rather than a
ranking. Dictionary methods retain an advantage these results do not
overturn --- every score they produce can be traced to the words that
generated it --- and that transparency is worth more than accuracy in
some research designs. What the evidence here settles is narrower and,
for non-anglophone researchers, more useful: the language of the prompt
is not among the things that need to be traded off.
